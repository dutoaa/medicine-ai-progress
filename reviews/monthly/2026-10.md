## Executive Summary

The supplied October 2026 papers suggest a shift in AI medicine from isolated prediction and fluent answer generation toward evidence-grounded, longitudinal, and operationally useful systems. Knowledge graphs, explicit citations, guideline-derived decision graphs, and deterministic calculation tools aim to make clinical outputs traceable. New benchmarks examine patient-specific reasoning across care phases, visual grounding, and agent workflow execution rather than relying solely on examination accuracy. EHR foundation models increasingly address cross-system transfer, screening-relevant operating points, and training design, while imaging research emphasizes multimodal integration, structured reporting, and resource efficiency. Privacy, demographic bias, uncertainty, and population representation also receive sustained attention. These are promising research directions rather than evidence of widespread clinical readiness: many supplied abstracts are truncated, limiting assessment of results, validation protocols, and clinical benefit.

## Major Themes

- Evidence-grounded clinical reasoning: MM-KG, GLoC-EHR, BAR, and pathology knowledge frameworks connect model outputs to patient observations and external biomedical knowledge.
- Evaluation beyond final-answer accuracy: NPC-RubricBench, MedHarnessBench, and RetinaGroundBench assess longitudinal responsibilities, workflow execution, and localization of supporting image evidence.
- Longitudinal and transferable EHR modeling: TRACER, Asymmetric SupCon, GraphBEAT, and sequence-composition research address temporal prediction, heterogeneous records, and cross-system generalization.
- Hybrid decision architectures: System One models, guideline-derived decision graphs, and executable medical calculators separate probabilistic interpretation from constrained or deterministic operations.
- Interactive clinical agents: ClinVerse and treatment-planning research move toward active information gathering and sequential decisions, while operational frameworks address staffing and real-world study automation.
- More interpretable multimodal imaging: Criteria-based diagnosis, longitudinal report generation, and image-text fusion seek to connect predictions with clinically meaningful findings.
- Safety and equitable deployment: Counterfactual triage audits, patient-level privacy analysis, multilingual datasets, and locally collected imaging data broaden the assessment of deployment risks.
- Resource-aware medical AI: Compact segmentation models, local decision models, and budget-constrained knowledge retrieval explore alternatives to uniformly scaling model size and inference cost.

## Field Progress

- Clinical evaluation is becoming more patient- and process-specific. Longitudinal rubrics, visual grounding annotations, and agent execution traces provide ways to identify failures that aggregate answer accuracy can conceal.
- Traceability is increasingly treated as an architectural requirement. Explicit evidence edges, cited EHR events, machine-checkable claims, and executable guideline paths offer more inspectable outputs, although traceability alone does not establish correctness.
- Foundation-model research is engaging more directly with deployment conditions. External health-system evaluation, cross-national transfer, and screening-relevant specificity targets better reflect practical use than single-dataset discrimination alone.
- Clinical AI architectures are becoming more modular. Fast decision models, language-model orchestration, constrained extraction, and deterministic tools allocate different tasks to components suited to their requirements.
- Safety assessment is broadening beyond hallucination. Patient-level privacy vulnerability, counterfactual demographic sensitivity, authorization boundaries, and uncertainty-aware operational decisions are becoming explicit research targets.
- The evidence base remains uneven. Large retrospective studies and detailed benchmarks coexist with conceptual perspectives and preliminary frameworks; the supplied material does not establish prospective clinical effectiveness for most approaches.

## Open Questions

- Do explicit citations, knowledge-graph links, and machine-checkable claims reduce clinically consequential errors, or primarily make existing errors easier to inspect?
- How well do longitudinal reasoning rubrics, visual grounding scores, and agent workflow metrics predict clinician performance, patient outcomes, and safe real-world use?
- Can cross-system EHR models maintain calibration and useful screening performance across changes in coding, prevalence, clinical practice, and patient demographics?
- What governance and verification mechanisms are needed for clinical agents that acquire information, invoke tools, share experience, or update external memories after deployment?
- How should health systems jointly evaluate privacy, fairness, uncertainty, workload, and cost when a model improves average performance but creates concentrated risks for particular patients?
- Which modular combinations of compact decision models, generative models, and deterministic tools deliver the best prospective clinical value under resource constraints?

## Outlook

The next meaningful step is to test whether more grounded and modular systems produce safer decisions and better workflows, rather than simply more convincing explanations. Priority studies should combine external and prospective validation with calibrated uncertainty, patient-level safety audits, clinician review, and endpoints such as unnecessary procedures, missed deterioration, workload, and resource use. Benchmarks should preserve decision-time evidence boundaries and distinguish model failures from retrieval, tool, and runtime failures. The supplied papers point toward clinical AI that is more inspectable, temporally aware, and operationally integrated, but establishing dependable benefit will require evidence beyond retrospective performance and framework design.
