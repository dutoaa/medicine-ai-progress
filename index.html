Variantscape: Retrospective Study of Large Language Model-Based Extraction of Variant-Treatment-Cancer Associations from Published Oncology Abstracts

Wosny, M.; Blindu, A. S.; Boesch, M.; Peres, T.; Niederhauser, T.; Fruh, M.; Rothermundt, C.; Hastings, J.

ObjectivesPrecision oncology relies on accurate interpretation of tumour-detected gene variants, to guide personalised treatment decisions. However, interpretation of variants in context requires extensive information that is often buried within unstructured biomedical literature and obscured by inconsistent nomenclature, making manual retrieval labour-intensive and prone to omissions. In this study, our aim was to evaluate the potential of large language models to extract variant-treatment-cancer associations at scale from published abstracts. DesignWe developed Variantscape, a large-scale, automated pipeline and open-access web tool. It integrates traditional natural language processing methods with state-of-the-art large language models to extract, standardise, and analyse co-associations between genetic variants, cancer types, and therapeutic interventions from published biomedical abstracts. ResultsFrom over 3.7 million abstracts published between January 2014 and October 2026, 886,195 articles containing gene names were eligible for extraction. Among these, 37,895 (4.3%) mentioned a variant, cancer type, and therapeutic agent together, covering 11,985 unique variants across 197 cancer types and 547 therapeutic agents. LLM verification against the abstract text confirmed at least one association in 35,224 papers, giving a literature-derived graph of 10,571 nodes and 44,644 verified edges. Canonical alterations in well-studied cancers (e.g., BRAF V600E in melanoma) were strongly linked to established treatments, while several rare variants also emerged with high-confidence literature support. ConclusionsBy applying large language models to biomedical literature, Variantscape enables scalable, context-aware extraction of trilateral variant-treatment-cancer relationships. This approach supports early evidence synthesis/hypothesis generation, highlights underrecognized or rare associations for MTB preparation, and offers a practical resource for accelerating discovery and supporting precision oncology research and translation. FundingThis study was funded by the School of Medicine at the University of St. Gallen in Switzerland under grant number 2300380. Article SummaryO_ST_ABSStrengths and limitations of this studyC_ST_ABS[bullet] Variantscape combines traditional natural language processing with large language models to enable scalable, automated extraction of variant-cancer-treatment relationships from over 3.7 million biomedical abstracts, substantially reducing the manual search burden inherent to existing molecular tumour board workflows. [bullet]The pipeline applies large language model-based inference to standardise inconsistent genetic nomenclature across the literature, improving the comparability and reliability of extracted associations that would otherwise be obscured by terminological variation. [bullet]Variantscape is designed as a continuously updatable, open-access resource, meaning it can incorporate newly published evidence without requiring repeated manual intervention. [bullet]The pipeline relies solely on abstract-level text rather than full-text articles, which may limit the depth and granularity of extracted associations, as key methodological details, variant context, and nuanced clinical findings are frequently reported only within the body of published papers. [bullet]Automated extraction using large language models introduces a risk of misattribution of variant-cancer-treatment relationships, and without systematic manual validation of outputs, the precision of identified associations at scale remains difficult to fully characterise.

Deep learning grading of Schistosoma japonicum-associated liver fibrosis from ultrasound images: a weighted soft-voting ensemble of convolutional and transformer networks

Guo, Z.; Ockenden, E. S.; Wei, M.; Li, Q.; Li, S.; Xue, J.; Li, Y.; Lin, D.; Zhou, X.; Chami, G. F.; Li, S.

BackgroundSchistosomiasis morbidity still persists in China despite extensive progress towards elimination of new and active infections. Diagnosis of liver fibrosis related to Schistosoma japonicum remains challenging due to the subjective nature of ultrasound grading and the shortage of experienced sonographers in endemic counties. MethodsWe developed a deep learning model that grades schistosomal liver fibrosis from a single B-mode ultrasound image without manual delineation of a region of interest. We used 2,921 images from 2,921 adults (aged 18-88 years, median age 54 years; 46.4% female) from a schistosomiasis hospital in Jiangxi Province, China, between 2019 and 2023. Of these, 12.9%, 34.6%, 32.4% and 20.2% had grade 0, 1, 2 and 3 fibrosis, respectively. Fibrosis grades were based on the clinical ultrasound record and re-evaluation by an experienced radiologist according to the Chinese Hand-book of Schistosomiasis Control; discordant cases were reviewed by a second radiologist and resolved by consensus. Predictions from four neural networks with different architectures (ResNet-18, EfficientNet-B0, DenseNet-121 and Swin-T) were combined by weighted soft voting, with weights derived from validation performance. ResultsFor the four-grade task this model achieved a macro-F1 of 0.8527 (95% CI 0.8184-0.8856), an accuracy of 0.8500 (0.8136-0.8818) and a quadratic weighted kappa of 0.9187 (0.8983-0.9373) on a fixed test set of 440 images. For detecting significant fibrosis (grade 2 or 3; test-set prevalence 0.530), sensitivity was 0.9700, specificity was 0.9179, positive predictive value was 0.9300, negative predictive value was 0.9645, and AUROC was 0.9817. The ensemble achieved a higher macro-F1 than each individual backbone. Every ensemble misclassification was to an adjacent grade; no image with severe fibrosis was classified as no or mild fibrosis, or vice versa, whereas each individual backbone made 1-4 errors spanning two or more grades. ConclusionsThe model may provide an automated aid for Schistosoma japonicum-associated fibrosis grading and could potentially support screening and follow-up in endemic areas after external validation.

Evaluating the Incremental Value of AI-Derived ECG Features Beyond Automated ECG Reports and Clinical Data for 30-Day Mortality: A MIMIC-IV Cohort Study

Park, S.-H.; Ji, Z.; Lewey, J.; Tsui, F.

Automated electrocardiogram (ECG) reports and clinical records already carry prognostic information, so the relevant question is what artificial intelligence (AI)-derived ECG waveform features add beyond these existing sources, and whether models with similar overall discrimination would prioritize the same patients for review. In linked Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) data, we compared 15 prespecified ECG morphology measurements with features from a previously trained deep-learning model for 30-day all-cause mortality in the same 25,430 held-out patients (924 deaths). Both feature sets fed identical prediction models developed and evaluated with the same procedures; paired 95% confidence intervals quantify each difference, basic clinical history -- age, recorded sex, and prior-diagnosis indicators -- was held constant where indicated, and analyses are labelled prespecified or post hoc. The AI-feature model ranked 30-day mortality risk better than the morphology model (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.812 versus 0.737; +0.075, 95% CI +0.059 to +0.091), and the advantage persisted with basic clinical history (+0.053, +0.039 to +0.067). In post hoc comparisons with basic clinical history held constant, replacing the expanded automated report associated with the index ECG with AI features changed discrimination little (+0.0037, -0.0053 to +0.0126), and adding AI features to the report-based model yielded +0.0075 (-0.0005 to +0.0151); both intervals include zero. At a hypothetical fixed 5% review capacity, the prespecified ECG-only comparison identified 236 versus 163 subsequent deaths (AI features versus morphology measurements), and with basic clinical history the corresponding post hoc comparison identified 262 versus 221. In a separate post hoc comparison using separately refitted models, the AI-feature and expanded-report review lists captured 262 versus 261 subsequent deaths (paired difference +1, 95% CI -25 to +22), yet shared only 654 of their 1,272 flagged patients: similar totals, different patient lists. One-year transport to a Brazilian cohort absent from the models earlier training retained the advantage over morphology measurements. These retrospective risk rankings measure prioritization, not prevented mortality: the advantage over richer routine reports was small and uncertain, and equal-capacity lists flagged different patients, so comparator strength and patient selection merit evaluation alongside discrimination before clinical use.

IntroductionCOVID-19 has been linked to neuropathology, neurological symptoms, and diagnoses of neurological illnesses. Evidence regarding the long-term impact of COVID-19 on neurodegenerative and neurological disorders is still limited. MethodsWe investigated associations of COVID-19 with neurodegenerative and neurological disorders by vaccination status in both hospitalised patients and the general population in the largest and longest study to date. We used OpenSAFELY-TPP to access linked electronic health records from 18.3 million adults registered with English GPs. We defined three cohorts with up to 5.5 years follow-up: pre-vaccination (exposed to COVID-19 January 2020-June 2021), and vaccinated and unvaccinated (exposed June-December 2021). We estimated adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) comparing the incidence of cognitive impairment and dementia after diagnosis of COVID-19 with before or without COVID-19. We also studied Parkinsons disease, restless leg syndrome, rapid eye movement sleep disorder, motor neuron disease, multiple sclerosis, and migraine. ResultsIncidence of most outcomes was elevated during weeks 0-4 after COVID-19 (cognitive impairment/dementia aHRs; pre-vaccination: 4.02 (3.74-4.32)/5.05 (4.63-5.52), vaccinated: 2.24 (2.05-2.45)/2.05 (1.77-2.37), unvaccinated: 4.38 (3.45-5.55)/8.69 (6.13-12.32)). Incidence remained elevated up to 4 years after COVID-19, especially in those hospitalised with COVID-19 (cognitive impairment/dementia aHRs; pre-vaccination: 1.49 (1.35-1.64)/ 1.39 (1.21-1.60), vaccinated: 1.27 (1.04-1.54)/1.32 (1.02-1.71), unvaccinated: 1.55 (1.11-2.16)/1.48 (1.00-2.20)). ConclusionIncidence of cognitive impairment and dementia were elevated shortly after COVID-19. Incidence of cognitive impairment remained elevated in the longer term, particularly among unvaccinated individuals and/or those hospitalised with COVID-19. These patients may require additional assessment during routine care to evaluate persistent or new cognitive concerns.

Applications of large language model in general practice: a qualitative interview study with professionals and patients

ter Heerdt, Y.; Bischoff, E.; Yang, J.; Timmers, M.; Jung, J.; van Linschoten, R.

BackgroundWorkload is increasing in general practice. Large language models (LLMs) are a promising tool that could relieve part of this workload. However, existing studies offer little insight into how professionals and patients want specific LLM applications to be implemented in practice. ObjectivesThis study aimed to prioritize a broad range of LLM applications in general practice and identify the benefits, risks, and requirements of each application. MethodsWe conducted semi-structured one-on-one interviews with 12 general practitioners (GPs), 7 GP assistants, 6 practice nurses, and 6 patient representatives in the Netherlands between October and December 2025. To guide the interviews, we developed a list of 21 LLM applications derived from literature, existing tools, and GP input. Application were divided into 5 categories: administrative tasks, pre-consultation and triage, clinical decision support, management of (chronic) diseases, and patient interaction and communication. Participants discussed and prioritized each application. Transcripts were analyzed with a combined deductive and inductive approach. ResultsPriorities for LLM applications differed by group: GPs favored clinical decision support, assistants favored triage, and practice nurses and patient representatives favored chronic disease management. The most prioritized applications were summarizing consultations and triage and urgency classification (high priority 18/31; 58%), followed by clarification of medical information around the consultation (14/31; 45%). The qualitative analysis revealed three recurring considerations when applying LLMs for different tasks. First, participants questioned whether an LLM is needed, as simpler or existing tools may suffice. Second, they questioned whether LLMs relieve workload or create extra work. Third, a possible benefit on workload has to explicitly be weighed against risks such as mistakes, reduced personal care and accessibility, and negative effects on professionals. ConclusionsProfessionals and patients see potential for LLM support across many areas of general practice. Realizing this potential requires three steps: critically assessing whether an LLM is the right tool, establishing that it relieves workload, and designing applications so that their benefits outweigh the risks. Our application-level prioritization and analysis provide a starting point for assessment and design.

A decision-only model as a low-cost judge of medical AI answers

Omar, M.; Glicksberg, B. S.; Sakhuja, A.; Barash, Y.; Charney, A. W.; Klang, E.; Nadkarni, G. N.

BackgroundTesting and deploying medical AI systems at scale already results in more outputs than clinicians can review, and the use of large language models (LLMs) as judges is growing rapidly. Decision-only models, cheap and fast models that return structured probabilities rather than text, offer a potentially scalable option for that goal. MethodsIn an OSF pre-registered evaluation, we tested TypeSafe Jev 1.13 as a judge on tasks from five validated clinical benchmarks: rubric grading (HealthBench), planted clinical errors (When Rubrics Fail), risk in AI-written clinical documents (MedVAL), counseling ratings and failures (CounselBench) and physicians preferences between answers (Real-POCQi). Comparators were expert raters agreement with each other, OpenAI Decisions, GPT-6 Luna, a proposed hybrid team combining decision models and a cheap LLM and, as frontier comparators, GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5. The main outcome was plain agreement with the expert label. ResultsThe test comprised 25,091 units and 213,913 model calls. Jevs agreement with expert labels averaged 64.7% over six tasks. On 3,614 matched units it averaged 66.1%, against 65.0% for Opus and 60.7% for Sol, and it was non-inferior to both on four of six tasks. Jev judged the whole pool in 17 minutes for US$1.46; per judgment, it cost 1/34 of Sols price and 1/152 of Opuss. A preset cutoff held its false-alarm rate on fresh planted-error answers, and in simulation, reviewing Jevs least-sure answers first found 1.4 times the errors of a random audit. Still, omissions were hard for both: at the same false-alarm rate, Jev missed 55.6% of planted omissions and Opus 48.1%, and Opus caught 8.8 points more planted errors overall. ConclusionsAcross more than 200,000 model calls, a decision-only model was non-inferior to two frontier judges on most tasks at a small fraction of their cost and time, while reporting how sure it was of every call. With local cutoffs and expert oversight of omissions and clinical documents, judging every output of a deployed medical AI system becomes more affordable.

ECG foundation models share an age-related performance disparity that originates in the data, not the models

Lung, D.; Breyer, M.-K.; Moscato, F.; Haberbusch, M.

BackgroundElectrocardiogram (ECG) foundation models are proposed for clinical use and promoted as fair, yet such claims are rarely tested with subgroup-stratified evaluation. We measured demographic disparities across four ECG foundation models, tested whether mitigation removes them, and asked whether the dominant disparity originates in the models or in the data and the clinical task. MethodsWe probed frozen embeddings of four architecturally distinct ECG foundation models (ECGFounder, xECG, ECG-FM, DeepECG-SSL) in thirteen cohorts on four continents (5.4 million analysed ECGs, 31 endpoints, six held-out cohorts), stratified by age, sex and race. We examined embedding geometry, tested post-hoc correction, larger labelled training sets, comorbidity exclusion and low-rank fine-tuning, and compared each network with an untrained copy of itself and with logistic regression on classical ECG measurements. FindingsDisparities concentrated on age, not sex or race (median worst-group AUROC gap 0.028 vs 0.004 and 0.016; age exceeded sex in 97% of results) and replicated externally (ED-BWH 30-day MACE AUROC 0.81-0.90 under 40 years, 0.64-0.71 at [&ge;]75). Age formed a smooth gradient in the embeddings, whereas sex, though most decodable, carried no gap. No remedy closed it (low-rank adaptation: -0.007 to -0.004). Untrained copies (r=0.81, 102 pairs) and classical measurements ({rho}=0.90) reproduced the endpoint-specific gaps, and pretraining raised AUROC (+0.067) without changing them. The largest race effect, higher potassium error in Black patients, vanished at matched true potassium. InterpretationECG foundation models carry an age-structured disparity that replicates externally and survives mitigation. Because untrained networks and a classical model reproduce it, the results indicate that it is inherited from the age structure of the ECG and its labels rather than created by the models, so switching or adapting models will not remove it. Age-stratified reporting, untrained and classical baselines, and age-specific thresholds and recalibration are therefore needed. Because the gap persists across architectures, vendors, fine-tuning and even untrained networks, switching models will not resolve it. Evaluations should include an untrained and a classical baseline before attributing a gap to a model, and services deploying these models should use age-specific thresholds and recalibration, or at minimum report and monitor performance by age band.

Ophthalmic AI With Just Ten Examples: Comparing Multimodal Large Language Models, Vision Foundation Models, and Vision-Language Models

Ramakrishna, P.; Strand, J.; Song, S.; Tavakoli, M. B.; Zetumer, S.; Majidi, S.; Jiang, Y.; Shen, M.; Berni, A.; Gregori, G.; Rosenfeld, P. J.; Kass, M.; Gordon, M.; Lee, C. S.; Kihara, Y.; Lee, A. Y.

PurposeTo compare multimodal large language models (MLLMs), vision foundation models (VFMs), and contrastive vision-language models (VLMs) for ophthalmic image interpretation with no labeled examples (zero-shot) or as few as 10 labeled examples per class (10-shot). DesignRetrospective benchmarking study. Subjects4 public datasets (UCSD, IDRiD, REFUGE, ASOCT-MSDC) and 3 private datasets (OHTS, BPEI-DLS, WashU-OCT) of anterior-and posterior-segment ophthalmic imaging. MethodsNine AI models were evaluated across 3 architectural families: MLLMs (Qwen3.8-27B, GLM-5.3-Flash, Gemma-4-31B-it), VFMs (DINOv3, RETFound, VisionFM), and VLMs (CLIP, EyeCLIP, SigLIP2). Models were assessed on disease classification, disease risk and severity grading, anatomical assessment, and lesion localization in fundus and OCT imaging. MLLMs were also compared with a 3D VFM (OCTCube-M) for whole-volume OCT classification. MLLMs were evaluated zero-shot and 10-shot, without additional training. Vision models (VFMs and VLMs) were adapted by linear probing and fine-tuning on the same 10-shot set and on the full training dataset (FD). Main Outcome MeasuresDisease classification performance (macro-averaged F1, AUROC, quadratic weighted kappa [QWK]), anatomical localization accuracy, and cross-dataset generalization. ResultsFor retinal OCT disease classification, 10-shot MLLMs achieved a macro-F1 of 0.94, versus 0.59 (10-shot) and 0.96 (FD) for vision models. For diabetic retinopathy and macular edema grading, 10-shot MLLMs achieved QWKs of 0.69 and 0.73, versus 0.48 and 0.57 (10-shot) and 0.72 and 0.79 (FD) for vision models. For lesion localization, 10-shot MLLMs achieved an F1 of 0.75, versus 0.70 (10-shot) and 0.86 (FD) for vision models. For vertical cup-to-disc ratio estimation (OHTS), zero-shot MLLMs achieved a mean absolute error of 0.14, versus 0.21 (10-shot) for vision models. For whole-volume OCT classification (WashU-OCT), zero-shot MLLMs achieved a macro-F1 of 0.71, versus 0.50 (10-shot) and 0.82 (FD) for vision models. ConclusionsOpen-weight MLLMs given 10 labeled examples per class outperformed VFMs and VLMs trained on the same examples in most tasks and approached the performance of fully trained VFMs and VLMs in selected disease classification and grading tasks. MLLMs also demonstrated promising zero-shot performance on non-public clinical datasets without task-specific training. By reducing the need for large labeled datasets and task-specific model adaptation, MLLMs could broaden who can develop and validate ophthalmic AI.

Comparing Machine Learning Methods for Detecting Caregiver Burden in Transcribed Conversations with Clinicians

Klein, A.; He, W.; Chen, H.; Tong, B.; Summerhayes, E.; Shen, L.; Hodgson, N.
