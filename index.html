RESCUE: An end-to-end multi-agent LLM system for proactive rare-disease patient screening in the EHR

Liu, C.; Geltzeiler, A.; Afyouni, A.; Nie, M.; Ravi, K.; French, C.; Chung, W.; Wojcik, M. H.

Rare diseases affect a significant portion of the global population, yet patients often endure a lengthy diagnostic odyssey and miss timely diagnosis with exome or genome sequencing (ES/GS). Existing informatics tools often rely on pre-identified patients or institution-specific rule sets that are laborious to implement locally and rarely address clinical utility and operational feasibility. We introduce RESCUE, an end-to-end, multi-agent LLM-powered workflow that automates the local implementation of both rule-based and machine learning screening algorithms across the entire electronic health record (EHR). Four specialized agents operate in sequence: the Ontology Agent classifies clinical data into a four-tier genetic-evidence taxonomy; the Modeling Agent builds a positive-unlabeled (PU) XGBoost classifier; the Screening Agent applies the model across the EHR and flags patients with a rule-based algorithm; and the Review Agent samples clinical notes to assess medical necessity and operational feasibility. In a retrospective evaluation at a pediatric hospital, the model showed strong discrimination on a holdout set (n=12,591; AUC 0.808). Of 175,842 patients meeting screening criteria, those flagged by RESCUE were 7.4-fold more likely to receive subsequent genomic assessments than controls. Blinded chart review of this group confirmed previously missed, medically appropriate candidates for ES/GS with 80% precision, accounting for prior testing. By decoupling expert roles into modular agents, RESCUE offers a flexible, scalable framework, with machine-learning and rule-based approaches complementing rather than substituting for each other. It also provides a reproducible, agentic pathway for direct local implementation to reduce diagnostic delays on an institutional scale.

BenchmarkHub: an open-source platform for reproducible evaluation of large language models in healthcare

Alvarez-Arenas, J. I.; Jimenez-Carretero, D.; Sanchez-Cabo, F.

BackgroundLarge language models (LLMs) are solely judged by their scores on biomedical benchmarks, yet these scores are rarely reproducible since the prompts, inference settings and model builds are seldom reported. We developed BenchmarkHub, an open-source platform for auditable evaluation of language and vision-language models for biomedical research. MethodsBenchmarkHub is a Python web application (Django 5.2, SQLite) released under the MIT licence and available at https://github.com/juanjuanignacio/benchmarkhub. The application can be accessed through a browser interface, a set of command-line tools and a REST API, so interactive exploration and scripted pipelines produce identical, comparable records. The platform samples questions with fixed seeds, runs models directly or as tool-using agents in a code sandbox, grades answers with deterministic rules and stores every prompt, reply and tool call. The interface provides a leaderboard to rank performance of the models, allowing model comparison without any preexisting programming knowledge. FindingsWe used BenchmarkHub to evaluate 12 open-weight models in five experiments on 15 biomedical benchmarks (11 text, two multimodal and two computational biology) and two general benchmarks. The pooled accuracy ranged from 46{middle dot}1% (95% CI 43{middle dot}0-49{middle dot}3) to 80{middle dot}9% (78{middle dot}3-83{middle dot}3). Re-grading the responses with simple answer-extraction rules lowered a models accuracy by up to 33{middle dot}1 percentage points leading to a reorder of the leaderboard. Published scores were found for 23 out of the 143 benchmark/model combinations tested. The accuracy measured by BenchmarkHub was lower than in the published results in 19 out of the 23 available comparisons. The effect of four prompting protocols (1: default prompt; 2: asking the model to answer the letter only; 3: extended reasoning; 4: three-shot prompting) changed MEDQA accuracy by up to 11{middle dot}3 points but was not significant after multiple-testing correction with 150 questions. We also tested the impact of ablation on benchmarking using the MMMU benchmark dataset. Removing images lowered the accuracy by 18{middle dot}0 and 19{middle dot}3 points repectively for the two strongest models. Finally, the accuracy of agentic benchmarks using a sandboxed interpreter increased on computational biology tasks by 36-63 points. InterpretationBenchmarking is essential to assess the impact and validity of LLMs for medical applications. BenchmarkHub allows a fair and rigorous comparison of models across established datasets, including the possibility to upload own datasets. The suite includes visual tools to ease model comparison and ranking without any programming knowledge. Self-hosted infrastructure that records every choice and parameter makes benchmark claims checkable without data leaving the institution. FundingEuropean Union EU4Health Programme (JACARDI), Spanish Ministry of Science, Innovation and Universities-State Research Agency (MCIN/AEI) with FEDER funds, the European Union Recovery and Resilience Facility-NextGenerationEU, and the Instituto de Salud Carlos III. Research in context Evidence before this studyWe searched PubMed, arXiv and Crossref in October, 2026, for articles published from January, 2022, to October, 2026, without language restrictions, using combinations of the terms "large language model", "benchmark", "evaluation", "reproducibility", "prompt sensitivity", "medical", "clinical", "multimodal", "image", "agent" and "bioinformatics". A systematic review of 519 studies found that LLM evaluations in healthcare rely mainly on exam-style questions, rarely use real patient data and seldom report evaluation settings. Holistic frameworks and new task-specific benchmarks, including two published in this journal have broadened what is measured, but headline scores are usually produced by model developers with incompletely described protocols. Studies outside medicine show that prompt format, answer extraction and nominally deterministic decoding can each change benchmark accuracy substantially, and that many questions in general multimodal benchmarks can be answered from the text alone, without looking at the image. Much of this evidence comes from preprints or developer-run evaluations, and we found no medical study that quantified, on the same items and within one framework, the effects of prompting protocol, answer extraction, image availability and tool access, nor an open, self-hosted platform built for that purpose. Added value of this studyBenchmarkHub is, to our knowledge, the first open-source platform that combines a web interface, command-line and REST access, local and cloud model providers, a sandboxed tool-use agent loop, procedurally generated computational biology benchmarks and a complete per-question audit trail. Using only the platform and 12 locally served open-weight models, we show that the answer-extraction rule alone can change medical accuracy by more than the gaps between models and reorder them, that published scores are usually higher than those measured under a documented protocol, that protocol effects need larger samples than are typically used, that on a medical multimodal benchmark the strongest models kept about 90% of their accuracy without the images, and that tool access transforms performance on computational biology tasks. Implications of all the available evidenceMedical benchmark scores should be reported together with the protocol, answer-extraction rules, decoding settings, model build and input modality that produced them, and comparisons between models should be paired and made under identical conditions. Self-hosted evaluation infrastructure allows hospitals and research institutes to do this for their own candidate models without sharing data, as a reproducible screening step that precedes, and does not replace, clinical validation.

Systematic Benchmarking and Optimization of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for Clinical Phenotyping: A Type II Diabetes Case Study in an ICD-Screened Cohort

Park, H.; Rees, M.; Kruger, N.; Fuse, K.; Castro, V. M.; Gainer, V.; Wattanasin, N.; Benoit, B.; Wagholikar, K. B.; Murphy, S.

Traditional EHR phenotyping methods can require substantial disease-specific development and long-term maintenance. While integrating large language models (LLMs) with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is promising, the impact of RAG configurations remains underexplored. We systematically evaluated these design choices for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) phenotyping in an ICD-screened cohort. Using GPT-4o, we processed 170,678 clinical notes from 275 patients. We evaluated seven embedding models and three prompt strategies (baseline, improved zero-shot, and few-shot). Using the improved zero-shot prompt, we tested 350 embedding model-chunk size-retrieval depth combinations on the training set and selected eight optimized configurations for test-set evaluation. Combined with three prompts, these yielded 24 prompt-configuration combinations for comparison with ICD codes and the institutional PheNorm method. RAG achieved a mean sensitivity of 0.9019 (95% CI: 0.8866-0.9172), specificity of 0.7907 (0.7749-0.8066), PPV of 0.9405 (0.9365-0.9445), NPV of 0.6967 (0.6653-0.7281), and F1-score of 0.9204 (0.9124-0.9283). All combinations achieved higher PPV than ICD codes (0.7838). Compared with PheNorm, sensitivity was significantly higher (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001), while specificity differences were not significant. Paired bootstrap analyses showed higher NPV and F1-score, with no significant PPV difference. General-purpose embeddings showed higher sensitivity, whereas biomedical embeddings exhibited higher specificity and PPV. Few-shot prompting increased specificity and PPV but reduced sensitivity. Token usage varied substantially across embedding models. These findings suggest the importance of aligning RAG design with task-specific clinical priorities and token efficiency.

Automating postmarketing pharmacovigilance with large language model agents

Zhang, R.; Zhou, Y.; Chui, C. S. L.; Yu, D.; Wu, Z.; Luo, R.

Postmarketing pharmacovigilance relies on spontaneous adverse event reporting and labor-intensive observational studies, which suffer from underreporting and delayed risk detection. Large language model (LLM) agents could automate real-world evidence generation, but generalist agents make ad hoc analytical decisions that compromise epidemiological validity and reproducibility. Here we present MedCheckpoint, a multi-agent LLM system for automated postmarketing pharmacovigilance. MedCheckpoint grounds each analytical stage in a curated, source-traceable medical knowledge base, locks core study-design parameters before execution, performs patient-level analyses with human-written deterministic statistical tools, and enforces checkpoint rules that abort infeasible analyses, with optional human-in-the-loop review. We evaluated MedCheckpoint using a 54-question benchmark on a dual-purpose simulated electronic health record (EHR) testbed of 2.7 million individuals, designed with reliable clinical signals to evaluate routine analytical accuracy alongside spurious signals to stress-test system rigor. Across the benchmark, MedCheckpoint outperformed two coding-agent baselines in report quality across five dimensions rated by three independent LLM judges (overall means 4.81-4.88 versus 4.25-4.55 and 4.02-4.29) and yielded higher run-to-run consistency (mean within-question standard deviation 0.116-0.186 versus up to 0.440). Crucially, while baselines failed on up to 2 of 21 runs when handling infeasible comparisons or unmeasurable outcomes, MedCheckpoint produced no misleading failures across all seven stress-test trap questions with known data limitations. MedCheckpoint enables reliable, auditable, and scalable pharmacovigilance analysis.

Benchmark Self-Triage (BeST): Comparing Self-Triage Systems and Multiple Generations of OpenAI Models Against Preliminary Expert Consensus

Kroll, L. E.; Seidel, A.; Schuster, J.; Steiger, E.; Schoepke, T.; von Stillfried, D.; BeST Study Group,

As digital self-triage becomes more widely used, quality assurance requires evaluation of both emergency recognition and overall agreement with clinical judgement, including how these outcomes differ across generations of large language models. The Benchmark Self-Triage (BeST) project intends to provide a framework to compare systems for digital self-triage against a medical gold standard in Germany. As a first step in developing and demonstrating the methodology, we compared study-specific implementations of SmED 5.4.11 accessed via API and Infermedica Engine API via Microsoft Azure Healthcare Agent Service with five structured OpenAI configurations spanning multiple model generations: gpt-4.1, gpt-5.4, gpt-5.6-luna, gpt-5.6-sol, and gpt-6-astra. The manufacturers of SmED and Infermedica offer MDR-certified Class IIb triage products, but these certified products were not directly evaluated. An LLM-based patient actor covered 480 physician-curated vignettes derived from German routine triage data. A pool of seven physicians provided preliminary reference classifications and consensus for 480 vignettes. Recommendations were mapped to three urgency categories-emergency, acute, and not urgent-and evaluated using winsorised weights derived from routine triage frequencies. Weighted emergency sensitivity ranged from 51.3% (95% CI 35.566.3%) for gpt-4.1 to 99.9% (99.7-100.0%) for gpt-6-astra. Weighted exact three-category agreement (accuracy) ranged from 54.4% to 68.8%; gpt-4.1 achieved the highest agreement despite the lowest emergency sensitivity. No system performed best across all urgency categories. The later-generation OpenAI configurations showed a more precautionary triage profile resembling SmED, with higher sensitivity for emergency cases; gpt-6-astra showed the strongest shift. This cross-sectional comparison across model generations suggests a shift towards more precautionary triage behaviour, without establishing a longitudinal improvement in accuracy. These findings highlight the limitations of overall agreement for evaluating self-triage and support category-specific assessment. Broader expert consensus and evaluation, as well as further steps to reduce dependence on SmED-derived source data, are needed and planned before drawing conclusions about clinical safety.

Generative AI Versus Primary Care Physicians from Plan Initiation to Long-Term Maintenance of Chronic Disease Self-Management: A Vignette-Based Validation Study

Zhao, Z.; Yang, H.; Wang, Q.; Wang, Y.; Cheng, W.; Ma, X.

BackgroundGenerative artificial intelligence (GenAI) may provide personalised and dynamically adjustable support for chronic disease self-management. However, previous studies have largely focused on isolated questions and a limited range of evaluation dimensions. ObjectiveTo compare the quality of self-management advice generated by GenAI and primary care physicians (PCPs) across multiple stages of chronic disease management and evaluation dimensions. MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional, source-blinded comparative evaluation using 15 standardised vignettes of diabetes and hypertension derived from authentic primary care follow-up records. Each vignette comprised three sequential management stages, with medical accuracy, readability, and behaviour-change potential assessed across the three simulated stages; individualisation and multidisciplinary integration at plan initiation; contextual adaptability during acute adjustment; and empathy during long-term maintenance. The GenAI system and 22 PCPs independently generated advice which was independently rated by three clinicians, one each from general practice, endocrinology, and cardiology. across seven dimensions. Linear mixed-effects models were used for the primary comparisons, and case-level paired and physician-distribution analyses were conducted as robustness analyses. ResultsThe GenAI system achieved estimated marginal means above 4.0 in all dimensions except readability. It scored significantly higher than PCPs in six of the seven dimensions, with adjusted mean differences ranging from 1.16 to 1.56 points (P<.001). Readability was the only exception, with no significant difference between GenAI and PCPs (-0.22, 95% CI -0.54 to 0.11; P=.19). The GenAI mean exceeded the individual means of all recruited PCPs in the six dimensions but ranked at approximately the 23rd percentile for readability. ConclusionsOur findings indicated the GenAI system may generate more highly rated self-management advice than PCPs across most evaluated dimensions but offered no advantage in readability. These findings provide pre-implementation evidence for GenAI as a potential source of structured and adaptive chronic disease self-management support. Patient-level pilot studies and subsequent randomised trials are needed to determine its acceptability and effects on behaviour and clinical outcomes.

MENTOR: An Evidence-Informed Large Language Model-Based Multi-Agent System for Efficient Selection of Institution-Specific Negative Control Outcomes

Yang, T.; Chang, J.; Kim, Y.; Cho, J.; Choi, K.; Son, M.; Kim, C.

ObjectiveNegative control outcomes (NCOs) help detect and correct systematic error in observational studies, yet their selection remains largely manual. The Common Evidence Model (CEM) can generate candidate lists, but researchers must still screen them for plausibility as true nulls and for sufficient observation in each institutions data. We developed and evaluated MENTOR (Multi-agent Evidence-informed Negative conTrol Outcome selection with Reasoning), a multi-agent system of large language models (LLMs) that combines CEM evidence with LLM reasoning to select institution-specific NCOs. MethodsFour LLM agents operate on an institutions Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership common data model. A Curator summarizes drug labels, a Suggester assesses locally frequent candidates with CEM evidence and a first-pass LLM judgment, an Adjudicator resolves their disagreements, and a Diagnostician relaxes the patient-count threshold until a target number of NCOs is met. Using Qwen3.6-27B, we evaluated 349 candidates for three drug pairs in a Korean tertiary hospitals electronic health records (2.9 million patients) against a blinded LLM judge from a different model family, validated by three experts on 90 candidates. We compared accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and sensitivity with CEM-only and first-pass-LLM-only selection. ResultsMENTOR proposed 243 NCOs versus 121 by CEM-only, with higher accuracy (0.83 [0.79-0.87] vs. 0.53 [0.48-0.58]), sensitivity (0.89 vs. 0.41), and PPV (0.86 vs. 0.79). Judge-expert agreement was {kappa}=0.63. Adjudication, needed for 44.1% of candidates, added little accuracy, and only with reasoning mode. Concordant acceptances, which bypass it, contained about half of the false positives. Only 7%-9% of CEM-proposed outcomes had at least 10,000 patients at the institution. ConclusionCombining LLM judgments with CEM evidence and local data yielded more NCOs, with higher PPV, than CEM alone. Restricting reasoning to discordant candidates kept computation time below 40% of that with reasoning in all agents. Every decision comes with its evidence and rationale for investigators final review.

Ontology-Based SEA-CDM Integration for Vaccine Clinical Trials: Query, Similarity, and Design Recommendation

Zhu, J.; Yeh, F.-Y.; Zheng, J.; He, Y.

ClinicalTrials.gov provides valuable information for vaccine research, but fragmented data structures and inconsistent vaccine naming complicate systematic cross-trial analysis. The Study-Experiment-Assay Common Data Model (SEA-CDM) provides a standardized structure for biomedical study data, while biomedical ontologies supply semantic relationships among concepts. We extracted 9,147 vaccine-related study records from the Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov (AACT) database, mapped their records into SEA-CDM, and linked vaccine products and reported adverse events (AEs) to the Vaccine Ontology (VO) and Ontology of Adverse Events (OAE), respectively. Building on the existing Ontology-based SEA Network (OSEAN), we organized the integrated data into relational tables and a Neo4j knowledge graph while retaining links to original registrations. We evaluated three applications to assess semantic trial retrieval, context-aware AE ranking and trial similarity, and trial design recommendation. First, VO-based query expansion retrieved 41 candidate RNA vaccine trials compared with 16 using exact VO identifier matching. Second, a context-aware AE ranking model incorporating SEA-CDM features and VO-derived vaccine families improved recall@10 from 0.317 to 0.383 over a frequency-based baseline. TransE and CompGCN embeddings achieved Hits@10 of 0.950 and 0.946, respectively, for retrieving trials sharing the same VO identifier within disease-specific candidate sets. Third, an ontology-based clinical trial design recommendation model was developed using several machine learning methods under leave-one-Material-out evaluation across 23 vaccine classes, although performance varied across classes. These findings demonstrate how structural and semantic integration can support multiple clinical trial analyses using vaccine studies as a case study. This framework provides a foundation for extending ontology-supported data integration and analysis to other clinical research domains.

Sleep Variability From Routine Nursing Observation and Wearable Actigraphy and Post-Enrollment Length of Stay in Acute Psychiatric Inpatients: Prospective Observational Study

Chen, H.-M.; Chen, Y.-L.; Yang, C. C.-H.

BackgroundLength of stay (LOS) in acute psychiatric inpatient settings is clinically and operationally important, but practical early markers for identifying patients at risk for prolonged hospitalization remain limited. ObjectiveTo evaluate whether sleep instability measured during the early inpatient observation period was associated with prolonged post-enrollment hospitalization in acute psychiatric inpatients. MethodsThis observational study included 94 consecutive patients admitted to an acute psychiatric inpatient unit. Monitoring began after study enrollment, and participants contributed 5 to 7 available nightly observations from wrist actigraphy and routine nursing observation; observations were not required to occur on consecutive calendar days. The primary exposure was intraindividual sleep variability (IISV), defined as the within-subject standard deviation of nightly total sleep time across available post-enrollment observations. The primary outcome was prolonged post-enrollment length of stay, defined using the 75th percentile of the cohort distribution. Age- and sex-adjusted logistic regression models evaluated associations, discrimination, calibration, and risk stratification. Total length of stay from admission was examined as a temporally distinct sensitivity estimand. ResultsParticipants were enrolled a median of 4 days after admission (IQR 2-7) and contributed 5 to 7 available nightly observations, which accumulated over a median of 8 calendar days (IQR 7-10); 39 of 94 observation series (41.5%) fell on consecutive calendar days. Prolonged post-enrollment hospitalization was defined as more than 24.0 days, yielding 23 events among 94 participants. Greater actigraphy-derived IISV was associated with prolonged post-enrollment hospitalization (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 2.30 per 1-hour increase; 95% CI, 1.08-4.93; P=.03), with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.756 (95% CI, 0.626-0.852; optimism-corrected, 0.725) compared with 0.692 for a model containing age and sex alone. Nursing-derived IISV showed an association of similar direction (adjusted OR, 3.10; 95% CI, 1.15-8.39; P=.03; AUC, 0.723; 95% CI, 0.571-0.837; optimism-corrected, 0.690). A combined model containing both measures did not improve discrimination over actigraphy alone (AUC, 0.751). ConclusionsSleep instability measured during the early inpatient observation period, derived independently from wearable actigraphy and from routine nursing sleep documentation, was associated with prolonged post-enrollment hospitalization. Because the outcome interval overlaps the period during which the sleep observations were accumulated, these findings describe an association across the early inpatient course rather than a prediction made at enrollment. The results support further evaluation of repeated sleep variability as a longitudinal inpatient measure; the variable observation schedule, single-center sample, and absence of external validation mean that clinical implementation remains premature.

DxToniaNet: A Neural Network for Estimating the Severity of Leg Dystonia in Children with Cerebral Palsy

Both confidence intervals include the World Health Organization (WHO) triage thresholds (sensitivity 90%, specificity 70%). When a screening study with partial verification was added, pooled specificity increased to 0.735. Eighteen of 24 assessed evaluations were at high risk of bias and only 1 of 21 studies was prospective, fully verified, externally validated and met the WHO thresholds. Certainty of evidence was low for sensitivity and very low for specificity. A subsequent PubMed search found additional eligible clinical evaluations that were not screened, so the review is incomplete for those studies. In clinical cohorts, AI-CXR sensitivity is close to the WHO triage target, and specificity is close to, but not clearly above, its target. The evidence is pooled across only three cohorts, and specificity varies by product and setting. Thresholds need to be calibrated locally, and prospective, externally validated evaluations are needed before widespread deployment.
