Hx2Dx: From Anamnesis to Diagnosis - A Multimodal Arena for Conversational Clinical Reasoning in LLMs

Federico Ravenda, Mirko Cesarini, Daniele Montagnani, Volodymyr Karpenko, Antonietta Mira, Andrea Raballo

Large language models (LLMs) achieve expert-level diagnostic accuracy in static evaluations, where the model receives a curated vignette or case report that already condenses the diagnostically relevant findings. Such evaluations measure inference over already selected evidence; the evaluations do not account for the inquiry used to obtain it. In clinical practice, however, diagnosis is an iterative process of inquiry. Clinicians take the patient's history, or anamnesis (Hx), through targeted questioning, examine the patient, and request and interpret tests, progressively narrowing the differential until they reach a final diagnosis (Dx). We introduce Hx2Dx ARENA, an interactive benchmark of 1'000 clinical cases from the New England Journal of Medicine (NEJM). Each case is a simulated encounter that proceeds from initial presentation through history taking and physical examination to diagnostic workup. We evaluate open- and closed-source LLMs under multiple configurations. Performance varies considerably across models in the interactive setting, and every model loses diagnostic accuracy relative to the static setting, where the full case is presented upfront. The ability to ask the right questions accounts for much of both the gap between models and the loss of accuracy: diagnosis improves as questions become more targeted and build a more complete clinical picture. LLMs thus reason well once the relevant data are available but remain limited in hypothesis-driven information gathering, in knowing what to ask in order to know. Closing this gap is a prerequisite for their safe integration into real-world diagnostic workflows.

Among these, deep learning-based label-free virtual staining uses images of unstained tissue to computationally generate stained images. TrialDesignBench: Benchmarking Language Models for Phase-I Trial Design Against Clinical Knowledge

Yihan Hank Tang, Peng Yang, Jiayi Xin, Tianqi Shang, Xuyang Chen, Ying Yuan

AI is used in much of the clinical trial pipeline. However, selecting the statistical design of a trial, a crucial step that determines how drug doses are tested on humans and evaluated, has seen almost no AI progress. LLMs appear to be a natural fit given their strengths in biomedical knowledge and statistical reasoning. Nevertheless, there is currently no appropriate way to evaluate such abilities because recent regulatory and methodological shifts, most notably FDA's Project Optimus, have redefined what constitutes a reasonable design and rendered many previous designs obsolete. To lay the groundwork for AI-assisted trial design, we introduce TrialDesignBench, a benchmark of 617 Phase-I/I-II oncology dose-finding trials, each paired with an expert-validated reference design that is consistent with the latest FDA regulatory initiatives. This reference standard is produced through a formalization of the expert design process. We evaluate five frontier LLMs, none of which matches the reference standard. Our ablation experiments trace this performance gap to two main causes, inference of latent, unrecorded design determinants from trial sources (supplying them raises class-wise balanced accuracy by 0.22) and structured decision-tree reasoning (+0.24). TrialDesignBench offers a reproducible, contemporary-standard objective for evaluating and enhancing AI-assisted trial design.

BioMem: Agentic Memory for Biomedical LLM Agents

Mingchen Li, Yongkang Xiao, Rumeng Li, Rui Zhang, Hong Yu

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated considerable potential in biomedical applications, while agentic memory systems have emerged as a promising approach to preserving and utilizing information across tasks and interactions. However, a fundamental question remains: how effectively and reliably do these systems retain, retrieve, update, and utilize biomedical information? In this study, we systematically investigate agentic memory in the biomedical domain, focusing on task effectiveness, retrieval quality, information fidelity, and long-term robustness. We evaluate five memory approaches across four LLMs in three representative settings: biomedical knowledge reasoning, clinical prediction, and long-term conversational memory. Our analyses reveal how memory construction, evidence relevance, and conversational history shape downstream biomedical performance, highlighting the distinct challenges of retrieving relevant information, preserving its fidelity, and effectively utilizing it for reasoning. Building on these findings, we introduce Hybrid Memory Retrieval and Utilization (HMRU), a training-free framework that jointly enhances evidence accessibility and task-aware utilization to improve biomedical reasoning and long-term conversational memory. HMRU achieves an average relative improvement of 8.6% across biomedical question-answering and conversational memory benchmarks, with additional gains in selected clinical prediction tasks. It further reduces conversational answer errors by 24.9% and closes 48.0% of the performance gap to full-context inference. Our findings provide a systematic characterization of biomedical agentic memory and practical insights into developing more effective and reliable memory-augmented LLM systems.

An evidence-traceable benchmark of clinical reasoning in large language models

Zhiqun Zhao, Xiaodong Yang, Bruce Gu, Ming Li, Yi Fu, Song Li

Benchmark scores for medical large language models (LLMs) are difficult to interpret when scoring criteria cannot be traced to clinical evidence. Here we developed Med Evidence Bench (MEB; https://www.medevidencebench.org.cn/), comprising 1,536 physician-constructed items across three levels: guideline knowledge, standardized clinical scenarios and real-world laboratory scenarios. Each criterion stating a medical claim is linked to a versioned source-text span. Across ten LLMs, the Evidence Trace Score (ETS) was 0.975-0.997 for guideline knowledge, 0.292-0.658 for standardized scenarios and 0.182-0.447 for real-world scenarios, and rankings varied across levels. For real-world scenarios, scores for identifying clinical information (mean, 0.770) exceeded those for matching evidence (0.165) or formulating recommendations (0.160). On 1,296 criterion-level judgments, automated grading agreed with the majority vote of three physicians (macro-F1, 0.930; 95% confidence interval, 0.915-0.945), although agreement was lower for evidence application. These findings from Chinese-language materials indicate that guideline recall does not ensure patient-specific evidence use and do not establish clinical effectiveness.

Operation-aware Multimodal Clinical Decision Support System for ICU Discharge Risk Assessment

Guoqing Zhang, Rafik Hadfi, Takayuki Ito

Unplanned 30-day intensive care unit (ICU) readmission is a critical benchmark for evaluating healthcare quality. Most predictive models assume readmission risk depends solely on a patient's individual pathophysiological state, ignoring hospital operational context and resource capacity. In practice, discharge decisions are dynamic trade-offs between patient stability and bed turnover pressures. To address this gap, we develop an Operation-aware Multimodal Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) for ICU discharge risk assessment. The system combines multi-agent collaborative extraction with self-evolving retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to eliminate template noise from progress notes, while constructing departmental workload indicators alongside admission flow features, continuous vital signs, and sparse medical codes (ICD/DRG). Addressing severe class imbalance (about 10.5% readmissions) and alarm fatigue, we implement data normalization, class reweighting, and decision-threshold calibration. In 20 independent runs across 20,000 ICU stays from MIMIC-III, empirical findings demonstrate that: vital signs alone have limited predictive capacity under real-world class imbalance (PR-AUC of 0.1387 +/- 0.0086); directly concatenating raw text embeddings degrades decision tree performance (XGBoost and LightGBM PR-AUC drop to 0.3893 and 0.4030); whereas an end-to-end multimodal fine-tuning architecture (Transformer E2E FineTune) utilizing layer-wise parameter freezing and differential learning rates reverses text negative transfer, achieving the highest AUROC of 0.8288 +/- 0.0110, PR-AUC of 0.4373 +/- 0.0310, and F1-Score of 0.4757 +/- 0.0196. Operational indicators supply independent risk signals, laying an engineering foundation for high-utility clinical decision support tools.

Differentiable Inverse Rendering Recovers Intrinsic Properties from Intravascular Optical Coherence Tomography

Ajay Manicka, Karim Kadry, Sebastian Ruiz-Lopera, Maryam Alsharqi, Sanjana Arji, Samuel Ruiperez-Campillo

Medical images encode intrinsic tissue properties that indicate disease progression, yet these properties remain entangled with the system-dependent physics of image formation. Differentiable inverse rendering asks whether they are recoverable by inverting the imaging system's forward physics rather than through post-hoc correction. We address this in optical coherence tomography (OCT), focusing on the optical attenuation coefficient, a per-pixel marker of pathology and cellular composition. Conventional recovery relies on closed-form inversions limited to simplified light-tissue models, or learned methods coupled to specific imaging configurations. We instead recover per-pixel attenuation from a single OCT image by gradient descent through a differentiable physics-based forward model. We verified the method against analytical solutions, validated it on five in vitro phantoms, and applied it without modification to 29{,}269 clinical intravascular OCT images from 128 patients. The inversion needs no training data or segmentations and is portable across platforms and retrospective archives. Replacing the forward model or recovering another optical parameter leaves the optimization procedure unchanged. We demonstrate per-pixel recovery is tractable because gradient information scales to this high-dimensional problem, where gradient-free methods become intractable. Our work supports quantitative tissue characterization from OCT images and provides a framework for extension across imaging modalities and organ systems.

Ninety-Six Ways to Measure a Scribe: Fragmented Evaluation of Ambient Scribes in Healthcare

Amama Mahmood, Shiye Cao, Laura Fleig, Junan Jiang, Theodore Jensen, Yee-Yin Choong

Ambient artificial intelligence (AI) scribes have moved rapidly from experimental pilots to enterprise-wide deployment across health systems, promising reduced documentation burden and improved clinician experience. While research evaluating these tools has expanded quickly, there are no agreed-upon protocols or standardized metrics for assessing their performance, making it difficult to compare findings or cumulatively synthesize evidence across studies. Across 54 recent evaluation studies, we identify ten evaluation dimensions and 40 distinct metrics, operationalized in 96 different ways-35 of them (36%) used by only a single study. Assessment is dominated by documentation efficiency and time, measured in 44 of 54 studies. The other dimensions appear at broadly similar rates (16-24 studies each), and safety and clinical outcome least consistently of all. Ripple effects on the broader care system, such as impact on patients' care outcomes, redistribution of work, changes in billing and coding intensity, and downstream use of notes by other clinicians are rarely assessed. Moreover, the metrics used rely heavily on self-report and are fragmented and inconsistently operationalized, which limits comparability and hinders synthesis across studies. As an effort to support more rigorous and cumulative evaluation, in this paper we review the study designs, data sources, and metrics commonly used to evaluate ambient AI scribes in healthcare settings, discuss the limitations of current evaluation practice, and argue for a more holistic approach grounded in a standardized, multi-dimensional set of metrics.

Weight-Space Programming for Medical Imaging: Do We Still Need Training Data?

Maximilian Lindholz, Richard Ruppel, Annette Milnik, Sebastian Halskov, Semil Eminovic, Charlie A. Hamm

Can a large language model (LLM) write the parameters of a medical image classifier from medical knowledge alone, without any training data? Such classifiers normally require labelled images, which are costly and often unavailable. We investigate this question through weight-space programming (WSP), in which an LLM writes or adapts the weights and biases of a neural network directly, without optimisation on training data. Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra each generate the parameters of a small LeNet-style CNN in a single reply, without tool use and without access to any image. We compare these networks against zero-shot MedSigLIP and against the same architecture trained on labels using the Adam optimiser, across 7 classification tasks in four modalities: chest X-ray, breast ultrasound and abdominal CT (all from MedMNIST), and pelvic MRI (SynthUterus). The LLM-written networks perform above chance on all six MedMNIST tasks, with a mean test AUC of 0.719 for Opus and 0.711 for Astra. Both models outperform MedSigLIP on 5 of 6 tasks while using more than 10,000 times fewer parameters. However, the same network trained with Adam on only 256 labelled images achieves a higher AUC on every MedMNIST task (mean AUC 0.828). On SynthUterus, Opus attains an AUC of 0.764, whereas Astra falls below chance (AUC 0.136) owing to a systematic error. These results show that LLMs can write functional medical classifiers, but that they do not yet replace training data. We release the protocol, logs and all generated weights at https://github.com/MaximilianLindholz/weight-space-programming-medical.

AI in the Clinical Documentation Loop: Effects of LLM-Induced Note Bloat and Diagnostic Overcall on Downstream LLMs

Jinghui Liu, Anthony Nguyen, Taylan Gurgenci

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to process medical records for healthcare tasks such as clinical documentation. Because these tools also generate documentation, the relationship is now bidirectional: models both read from and write to the record. Understanding how LLMs behave when reading records that already contain LLM-authored content is therefore critical for their safe deployment. In this study, we examine how LLM-authored content in the medical record affects downstream LLMs that read the same record. We consider two types of LLM-induced artifacts: bloat, in which LLM-generated summaries accumulate in the record and change the length and provenance of its content over time; and distortion, in which LLMs subtly alter clinical findings rather than fabricating facts outright. As a case of distortion, we study diagnostic overcall (documenting an unconfirmed differential diagnosis in the record) and test whether it propagates to the reader LLM's output. Across seven reader models from four families, we find evidence of both effects. First, LLM-generated summary notes modestly but consistently degraded downstream hospital-course generation compared with copy-forward and templated text of matched length. Second, injected overcalls reappeared in downstream outputs at rates that varied by reader model, without detectable changes in standard quality metrics. These findings show the need to monitor AI-generated content in medical records and to account for it when those records are later processed by AI.

Decisions by Discrimination: Differential Evidence for Medical Reasoning

Yunpeng Xiao, Yuzhang Xie, Junhao Huang, Carl Yang, Xiao Hu, Kai Shu

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly studied for clinical decision support, and medical question answering provides a testbed for evaluating this capability. In particular, medical multiple-choice questions (MCQs) reflect a core challenge in clinical decision-making: plausible options often share overlapping evidence, while only a few decisive factors distinguish the correct option from incorrect ones. However, existing retrieval-based and reasoning-oriented methods mainly focus on supplying broadly relevant knowledge or eliciting reasoning paths, rather than identifying the compact differential evidence that separates confusing alternatives. In this work, we present a retrieval-grounded differential evidence framework for medical MCQs. Our method extracts option-specific pieces of differential evidence and organizes them into concise evidence prompts for answer selection. This design better aligns external knowledge with differential reasoning while reducing irrelevant context. Experiments show consistent accuracy gains across multiple datasets and target LLMs. Ablation studies further show that our framework outperforms both directly using retrieved evidence chunks and closed-source LLM-generated general hints, and that its improvements are not solely explained by answer-proximal guidance, but are consistent with differential evidence serving as a compact aid for reasoning over competing alternatives. Our findings also offer insights for medical reasoning and other decision-making settings with multiple possible choices.

Medical Image Segmentation: Foundation Model Adaptation versus Task-Specific Training

Foundation models pretrained on large natural-image collections are increasingly adapted for medical image segmentation, in the expectation that they will replace models trained for each task. Most comparisons, however, use data from the training distribution and a single task-specific baseline whose configuration is rarely justified, and therefore provide limited evidence of performance at new clinical sites. It also remains unclear how much of a foundation model must be adapted, and what determines this.
