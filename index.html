Fairness or Fallacy? Mapping the Landscape of AI Bias in Disease Detection

Ghulam Mudassir, Hameed Ullah Khan, Ahmad Sami Al-Shamayleh, Muhammad Faizan Khan, Muhammad Muqtasid, Moamin Abughazala

Artificial intelligence (AI) has several transformative effects on healthcare, like to facilitate clinicians in disease detection and to manage patient care with enhanced precision. Due to the increasing complexity of medical data and the growing volume of diagnostic images, the substantial computational power needed to process such information creates both opportunities and challenges. To accomplish this objective, numerous methodologies have been proposed to integrate AI into disease detection systems. All these methodologies consider different aspects of clinical diagnosis, considering key parameters such as the type of AI model employed, the nature of input data, and the population demographics being analysed. In this paper, we review different state-of-the-art techniques which have been proposed for various areas of disease detection, specifically focusing on bias identification and mitigation. The proposed solutions are distinguished based on the type of input data they utilize, the architecture of model, and type of bias (algorithmic bias, demographic bias, model bias, and deployment bias). The time frame chosen to filter out relevant studies is between 2015 and 2026. Eventually, we review 23 related articles out of 64,389 analyzed from seven different digital libraries. The obtained results guide us to reach a conclusion with respect to state-of-the-art selected studies. Furthermore, these results reveal significant research gaps that can assist researchers in addressing AI-related bias in medical diagnosis. By prioritizing explainability, data diversity, and fairness in algorithmic design, as well as equitable access for affected patient populations, they can utilize available healthcare resources more effectively.

From Clinical Risk Prediction to Operational Decisions: An Uncertainty-Aware Framework for ICU Staffing

Jiayan Yang, Ad Feelders, Arno Siebes

Advances in electronic health records (EHR) and large-scale critical care databases have enabled the development of machine learning models for the early detection of patient deterioration. While these models often achieve strong predictive performance for outcomes such as sepsis and mortality, most studies evaluate them purely on discrimination metrics. This leaves a critical gap: how to systematically translate probabilistic clinical risk estimates into downstream operational decisions under real-world constraints, such as shift rigidity and hiring delays. This study proposes an uncertainty-aware anticipatory staffing framework that bridges short-term patient risk prediction and staff planning without altering the upstream predictive model. Instead of relying on naive deterministic aggregation or fixed safety buffers, the framework explicitly models dynamic predictive uncertainty and evaluates extreme shortage exposure using Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). These aggregated, risk-calibrated demand signals are then embedded into an hourly rolling-horizon scheduling policy to generate delay-constrained staffing decisions. Evaluated on realistic trajectories from the MIMIC-IV database, the proposed framework demonstrates a favorable cost-shortage trade-off compared to deterministic and purely reactive baselines. Pareto frontier analysis illustrates how uncertainty-aware demand construction changes resource allocation under varying safety preferences, while stress testing confirms the framework's operational robustness even when subjected to systematic predictive bias in the form of calibration drift. By formalizing this translation layer, this work provides a principled, transparent pathway for safely integrating probabilistic AI outputs into high-stakes healthcare operational systems.

Unified Multiparadigm Fusion Intelligence for Rare Disease Diagnosis

Jian Yang, Peiling Liu, Songda Li, Yu Cao, Yuqing Feng, Lan Yu

Rare diseases (RDs) affect an estimated 300 million people globally, leaving patients to navigate a protracted diagnostic odyssey constrained by clinical scarcity and fragmented medical knowledge. Artificial intelligence (AI) has progressively advanced RD diagnostics, from symbolic methods and deep learning to large language model (LLM)-based systems. However, each paradigm carries structural limitations: symbolic methods are bounded by knowledge completeness, neural models sacrifice interpretability, and LLM-based systems are vulnerable to evaluation biases like data leakage. In this study, we introduce FusionRare, a unified multiparadigm fusion framework integrating knowledge-driven symbolic reasoning, deep-learning pattern recognition, case-based patient retrieval, and LLM-based heuristic inference via statistical meta-aggregation and knowledge-enhanced agentic reflection. Evaluated on 2,528 strictly isolated clinical cases, FusionRare achieves state-of-the-art RD prioritization, attaining a macro-averaged Recall @1 of 51% for phenotype-only diagnosis and 79% for phenotype-genotype joint analysis. These results represent decisive advantages over the advanced agentic system DeepRare, frontier LLMs including GPT 5.5, and specialized tools like PhenoBrain and LIRICAL under stringent anti-leakage protocols. A randomized, crossover clinician-AI validation demonstrated that FusionRare elevates the true diagnostic rank in 45% of decisions, improves physician confidence in 62%, and reduces time to diagnosis by 21%. These findings indicate that reconciling these orthogonal AI paradigms significantly enhances diagnostic capabilities, bridging the gap between computational benchmarks and actionable clinical reliability. More broadly, this work provides a replicable framework for deploying trustworthy diagnostic AI across complex, high-stakes, and underserved medical domains.

In medical imaging including Computed Tomography, Magnetic Resonance Imaging or Positron Emission Tomography, such models have various important use-cases including data augmentation, domain transfer, image reconstruction, inpainting, image augmentation and artifact correction. CODA: Cohort- and Drift-aware Foundation Model for Multimodal Clinical Reasoning

Changshuo Liu, Jiaqi Zhu, Wenqiao Zhang, Xiaokui Xiao, Beng Chin Ooi

Recent advances in multimodal foundation models have demonstrated strong performance across diverse clinical tasks. However, existing approaches predominantly operate at the individual sample level and fail to model the interplay between population-level cohort patterns and patient-specific variation that underlie real-world clinical reasoning. In practice, clinicians routinely reason through patient cohorts sharing similar demographic, physiological, or pathological characteristics, while accounting for patient-specific deviations from the group prototype. Motivated by this gap, we propose CODA, a cohort- and drift-aware foundation model for multimodal clinical reasoning. CODA explicitly integrates cohort structure into the modeling pipeline through two complementary mechanisms: cohort-aware population stratification and drift-aware individual adaptation. In particular, we leverage reinforcement learning to discover clinically meaningful patient cohorts, and further model each patient's deviation from cohort prototypes as a structured drift signal encoding fine-grained individual heterogeneity. These cohort- and drift-aware representations are incorporated into the foundation model to enable reasoning that is simultaneously informed by population-level patterns and sensitive to individual-level variation. Extensive experiments on real-world electronic health record (EHR) benchmarks demonstrate that CODA achieves consistent effectiveness and strong generalizability across diverse clinical tasks, including closed-QA, open-QA, and report generation.

TIM: Temporal Decoupling with Iterative Mutual-Refinement Model for Longitudinal Radiology Report Generation

Yiheng Dong, Yi Lin, Shilong Huang, Xiyan Yang, Xin Yang

Automatic radiology report generation (RRG) aims to translate medical images into diagnostic text, reducing radiologists' workload and standardizing clinical documentation. Nonetheless, existing approaches mainly focus on single-timepoint analysis and fail to capture temporal disease evolution across longitudinal examinations. While recent longitudinal RRG (LRRG) approaches incorporate historical data, they often combine images from different time points within a single representation space, leading to blurred semantics and inconsistent temporal reasoning. In this work, we propose a Temporal Decoupling with Iterative Mutual-Refinement Model (TIM), a two-stage framework that explicitly decouples spatial pathology from temporal progression and iteratively refines reports through mutual feedback. Stage I performs temporal-decoupled representation learning, separating temporal evolution patterns from disease-specific features and generating radiology reports for both prior and current studies. Stage II introduces a mutual report refinement mechanism that identifies diagnostic inconsistencies within prior reports and iteratively rectifies both prior and current reports through error-sensitive feedback. Experiments on the Longitudinal-MIMIC dataset demonstrate that TIM surpasses existing single-image and longitudinal baselines, achieving new state-of-the-art performance across both language and clinical metrics. Code is available at https://github.com/yihengd/TIM.

GraphBEAT: Graph-based Bucketized Event Aggregation Over Time for Early Risk Stratification of Gastrointestinal Cancers

Leo Y. Li-Han, Chris Varghese, Elizabeth B. Habermann, Mark J. Truty, William R. G. Perry, Cornelius A. Thiels

Longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs) contain complementary diagnostic and laboratory information about patient health. However, irregularity, sparsity, heterogeneity, and incompleteness in documentation complicate EHR modeling. To address the problem, we introduce GraphBEAT (Graph-based Bucketized Event Aggregation over Time), a temporal graph representation learning framework that groups clinical events into time buckets and transforms observed features into patient-specific, temporally directed graphs to enable early risk stratification for gastrointestinal cancers. Each node in an EHR graph encodes feature identity, observed value, and temporal position of clinical events. A context-aware, time-decay message-passing mechanism models transitions between successive buckets, followed by multi-head attention pooling to generate patient-level representations. GraphBEAT was trained and evaluated on multi-site longitudinal diagnosis codes and laboratory tests to predict pancreatic, liver, and intestinal cancer at 12-, 24-, and 36-month diagnostic lead times. In all experimental setups, GraphBEAT consistently outperformed baseline graph neural networks (GraphSAGE, GAT, and GCN) and sequence modeling approaches (Transformer and long short-term memory [LSTM]). GraphBEAT also required over 250x fewer floating-point operations per patient and over 20x fewer parameters than the Transformer and LSTM baselines. These results support GraphBEAT as an accurate and computationally efficient approach for cancer risk stratification from sparse, heterogeneous longitudinal health records.

DeliMF: Gated Cross-Attention Fusion of Structured Time Series and Clinical Text for Early Intensive Care Delirium Prediction

Yibo Yang, Zerui Huang, Haomiao Yang, Zixia Wang

Delirium affects a large fraction of intensive care unit (ICU) patients and foreshadows longer stays, lasting cognitive decline, and higher mortality. Flagging the patients who will become delirious within the first day of admission would give clinicians a window to intervene. Yet state-of-the-art predictors rely only on the structured electronic health record (EHR) of vital signs, laboratory values, and medications, while overlooking the free-text clinical notes written in the same window, which carry information the numbers cannot encode. To this end, we present DeliMF, a multimodal framework that fuses the structured time series with clinical text. It encodes the structured signals with a temporal Transformer and the time-stamped radiology reports with a clinical text encoder, and fuses the two through a gated cross-attention module. The gate learns a per-patient weight on the text; when a patient has no note the gate closes and DeliMF reduces to its structured-only form, giving robustness to missing text by construction. On an incident-only MIMIC-IV cohort, DeliMF improves significantly over the baselines, particularly on patients who carry clinical text, while losing nothing on those without. Ablation studies confirm the contribution of multimodal fusion and gated cross-attention, and a case study further shows that fusing clinical text with the structured stream recovers delirium cases that the structured data alone misses. We hope DeliMF encourages early ICU prediction to make fuller use of the overlooked clinical text and contributes to the early identification and timely intervention of delirium.

Shenqi Zhou, Qiran Zou, Wenhao Zhao, Zhenyu Liao, Dianbo Liu

Autonomous agents are increasingly capable of developing machine learning (ML) models end to end. In medical practice, however, strong performance on the target metric does not fully characterize how good the models are in real-world use cases. For example, medical ML models should also provide well-calibrated risk estimates and remain robust across patient populations. Yet autonomous model development is typically guided by task objectives that emphasize simple predictive discrimination (e.g. AUROC). This raises a key question: are these objectives sufficient to guide agents toward models that meet the broader requirements of real-world medical use? In this study, we introduce Auto-MD-bench, a benchmark of 15 real-world medical tasks from 10 clinical datasets, spanning survival analysis, classification and sequential prediction, with external validation across cohorts, hospitals and time periods. The strongest agents achieve competitive primary scores, yet their predicted risks are less calibrated than those of the simple baseline models, which could lead to severe medical accidents. Moreover, models with nearly identical primary scores can exhibit markedly different calibration, while performance varies substantially across population shifts. These findings show that primary task performance alone is insufficient to assess autonomous clinical modeling and multiple different metrics widely used in healthcare practice need to be taken into consideration.

TRACE: Tracking Multimodal Clinical Trajectories for Inpatient Risk Prediction

Minjun Kim, Jong Hak Moon

Accurate inpatient risk prediction requires modeling heterogeneous clinical evidence that accumulates irregularly over time. This challenge spans in-hospital mortality, prolonged length of stay, and readmission, each with task-specific observation windows. Existing multimodal approaches combine structured health record data, chest x-rays, and clinical text, but primarily focus on modality fusion or missing-modality compensation, with limited attention to temporal trajectories over an admission. We propose TRACE (TRAjectory modeling of multimodal Clinical Events), a daily-scale framework organizing clinical events into day-level trajectories, separating day-invariant global context from day-specific local events drawn from EHR data, chest x-rays, radiology reports, and discharge summaries. TRACE achieves the highest AUROC across all nine dataset-task settings and the highest ACC in eight, outperforming baselines. Ablation studies validate our attention masks and contrastive learning, while trajectory-level attribution localizes risk-associated hospital days and modalities.

Localization Gains from Explanation Regularization Should Not Be Interpreted as Faithfulness Gains.

Amrin Akter, Mauro Dalla Mura, Diana Mateus, Dawood Al Chanti

Training-time explanation regularization is often evaluated by whether saliency maps become more concentrated within annotated regions. We argue that this criterion alone is insufficient: transformation consistency, lesion localization, and faithfulness to the classifier are distinct properties and should be evaluated separately, particularly in medical imaging. We examine this distinction through a case study of Contrastive Grad-CAM Consistency (CGC) in three-class brain tumor MRI classification. Using a single-channel ResNet-50 with five-fold patient-grouped cross-validation, we compare a cross-entropy baseline with CGC. CGC changes overall Pointing Game (PG) from $27.24\%$ to $36.02\%$ and Relevance Mass Accuracy (RMA) from $11.17\%$ to $18.01\%$, while classification accuracy changes from $93.63\%$ to $94.87\%$. Perturbation-based faithfulness metrics show inconsistent changes: Insertion Area Under the Curve (IAUC) changes from $0.73$ to $0.78$, whereas Deletion Area Under the Curve (DAUC) favors the baseline ($0.36$ versus $0.41$; lower is better), and overall Iterative Removal Of Features (IROF) is $0.57$ for both models. These results support our position that improved spatial agreement with pathology should not be interpreted as a general improvement in explanation faithfulness. We therefore recommend property-specific evaluation in which the property targeted by the explanation objective, spatial agreement with reference regions, and model reliance are measured and reported separately.

Medical Fine-Tuning Reshapes, Not Reduces, Patient-Facing AI Risk: A Rubric-Based, LLM-Judge Evaluation Framework

Savan Doshi

Patient-facing medical question answering with large language models (LLMs) is moving from research demonstrations to production deployments, yet evaluation remains anchored on factual correctness. Factuality is necessary but not sufficient: a response can be factually grounded and still issue a directive that, if acted on, would harm the patient. We introduce a risk-sensitive evaluation framework that scores responses by the presence and severity of six categories of risk-bearing language (treatment directives, contraindications, dosage expressions, urgency-triage cues, high-alert medication mentions, and overconfident assertions), anchored on published clinical harm taxonomies. Severity is classified by a three-judge LLM ensemble. We apply this framework to six 7-9B instruction-tuned models, three general (Mistral-7B, Phi-3.5-mini, Llama-3.1-8B) and three medically adapted (BioMistral-7B, OpenBioLLM-Llama3-8B, Med42-Llama3-8B), on 200 real patient questions from the K-QA benchmark. Two findings emerge. First, ensemble risk-bearing language is statistically independent of factual hallucination rate (pooled Spearman $ρ= -0.065$, $n = 300$, 95% CI $[-0.18, +0.05]$; distance correlation $= 0.10$, independence test $p = 0.24$). Second, medical fine-tuning does not reduce aggregate risk but reshapes it: continued-pretraining adaptations (BioMistral, OpenBioLLM) consistently suppress high-alert medication mentions while amplifying overconfident assertions, whereas clinical instruction-tuning (Med42) suppresses dosing only, without amplifying overconfidence. Per-category Krippendorff $α$ is fair to good for the four linguistically anchored categories (0.53-0.71) and low for the two clinical-judgment-dependent categories (0.19, 0.31), indicating where automatic safety evaluation is and is not yet reliable. We release the rubric, judge prompts, and evaluation code.

When to Stop Asking: Factor Coverage Bias and LLM Determinability Judgments with Underspecified Information in Medicine

Yusuke Watanabe, Yohei Kobashi, Taisei Tosaki, Takeshi Kojima, Yusuke Iwasawa, Yasushi Okuno

Clinical decision-making often begins with underspecified information and requires not only gathering missing information but also knowing when to stop. This calls for judging determinability (whether the available information is sufficient for a decision) by considering whether missing information could change the conclusion, not simply the amount of available decision-relevant information. Reliance on this amount as a cue for determinability, which we term \textit{factor coverage bias}, could undermine the trustworthiness of large language models (LLMs) through premature conclusions or excessive information gathering, such as unnecessary invasive tests. Yet whether LLMs exhibit this bias remains insufficiently studied. To address this gap, we construct a controlled pairwise benchmark in which LLMs identify the determinable case within each determinable--undeterminable pair. We add a decision-relevant factor to either side while keeping each case's determinability unchanged, and similarly add irrelevant text to distinguish this bias from context-length effects.
