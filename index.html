ProCARE: Real-World Study Automation via Profile-Grounded Evidence Contracts

Zhaoyu Fan, Bowen Han, Jincheng Ou, Yanwei Ren, Zehua He, Kaiyu Huang

Real-world studies (RWS) analyze routinely collected healthcare data, such as electronic health records and insurance claims, to estimate treatment effects, monitor safety, and identify risk factors outside randomized trials. Automating RWS with large language model agents is appealing but brittle: valid analyses depend on dataset-specific clinical semantics, including index dates, follow-up windows, patient/event-level units, and outcome boundaries that are often implicit in observational data. This leads to schema mismatches, temporal errors, inconsistent analytical granularity, and unsupported clinical claims. We present ProCARE, a profile-grounded framework that reformulates RWS automation as constrained compilation. ProCARE converts heterogeneous data profiles into Evidence Contracts specifying available variables, temporal attributes, distributions, and evidentiary limits, and compiles study objectives into RWS-IR, a strongly typed intermediate representation for variables, time relations, analysis units, and statistical tasks. Deterministic validation and localized repair then check and revise generated workflows across schema mapping, cohort construction, variable derivation, analysis, and interpretation. On 36 EHR-based benchmark tasks, ProCARE outperforms strong LLM-agent baselines in code executability, semantic consistency, and conclusion traceability, with automated violation diagnoses showing moderate-to-substantial agreement with blinded expert review.

Task 1 asks participants to find the best strategy for aggregating knowledge from per-institution segmentation models, while Task 2 asks them to distinguish glioma from meningioma under the same federated protocol, in which the entity label is heavily confounded with the site of origin.

SMI: Ontology-Grounded Semantic IDs for Hierarchy-Aware Medical Concept Representation

Ziyang Song, Lia Shen, Yixuan Li, Qincheng Lu, Weihao Li, Haoran Xu

Biomedical language models (LMs) provide strong representations for clinical tasks, but they often fail to capture the hierarchical structure among medical concepts. This limitation is especially pronounced for rare and fine-grained clinical codes in long-tailed vocabularies, where hierarchy-agnostic LM representations become less informative as concept frequency decreases. We introduce Semantic Medical Identifier (SMI), a training-free framework for constructing ontology-grounded Semantic IDs and hierarchy-aware medical concept representations. For each diagnosis, procedure, or medication concept, SMI defines its Semantic ID as the coarse-to-fine path through an expert-defined medical ontology. SMI then aggregates frozen biomedical LM embeddings across the corresponding ontology levels to form hierarchy-aware medical concept representations that preserve coarse-to-fine clinical semantics. The base SMI uses uniform aggregation across ontology levels, while a level-weighted variant selects global level weights on validation data. Our theoretical analysis shows that SMI yields more informative medical concept representations than hierarchy-agnostic LM embeddings, particularly for rare and fine-grained clinical concepts. Experiments show that SMI captures meaningful hierarchical structure and improves clinical relation prediction. On MIMIC-IV, SMI improves mortality prediction, readmission prediction, drug recommendation, and rare drug prediction. On eICU, SMI yields more robust patient representations under cross-hospital distribution shift.

Sequence Composition in EHR Foundation Model Pretraining

Joshua Placidi, Inigo Stibbe, Alfred J. Balston, Marek Rei, A. Aldo Faisal

Autoregressive electronic health record (EHR) foundation models predict the next event token in patient trajectories: sequences of structured medical events. A typical pretraining strategy is to concatenate all trajectories into a global stream and sample token windows from it. This gives patients with longer sequences more training exposure and allows tokens to attend across patient boundaries. We argue that these choices create a misalignment between pretraining and downstream clinical tasks. To explore this, we investigate how the composition of pretraining sequences affects clinical prediction performance. Using the global-stream approach as a baseline, we propose alternative sequence composition methods that control how patient trajectories contribute to pretraining sequences. Across four MIMIC-IV v2.2 tasks, our best method improves macro AUROC, AUPRC, F1, and balanced accuracy, with relative gains of 9.3% in F1 and 2.7% in AUPRC under a fixed training recipe. We find that these gains generalize across four additional evaluation settings, including the INSPIRE and eICU-CRD datasets, with improvements in macro AUROC, AUPRC, and F1 score. Our results show that simply changing how EHR data are presented to the model during pretraining can improve downstream performance, without changing the model or collecting additional data.

PAINT: A Framework for Multilingual Pain Representation from Medical Licensing Exams to Real-World Clinical Texts

Boya Zhang, Yuanchao Ma, Jiageng Wu, Krittaphas Chaisutyakorn, Rodrigo Gameiro, Ahram Han

Pain is one of the most common and clinically important symptoms, reflecting disease onset, progression, and treatment response. However, pain is difficult to accurately capture and interpret from medical text, as its expression varies across languages, documentation types, and patient demographics, and is often embedded in terminology, negation, temporality, and contextual descriptions. Existing pain-related studies remain largely monolingual or limited to narrow clinical settings. In this study, we introduce PAINT, a multilingual framework for studying pain representation across medical exams and real-world clinical texts. PAINT includes nine pain-focused dataset partitions spanning English, Spanish, Portuguese, and Japanese, with clinician annotations in all four languages. We release the WorldMedQA-Pain ensemble labels, the extraction pipeline, validation scripts, and clinician annotation sheets for publicly licensed corpora, providing a foundation for pain-centered clinical NLP and for studying pain documentation across languages and healthcare contexts.

Reinforcement Learning In Partially Verifiable Environments for Colon Cancer Treatment Planning

Li-Ching Chen, Andrés Franco Valiente, Yuta Ishiyama, Zachary R. Chalmers, William Chen, Kelsey Natsuhara

Clinical decision-making is a sequential process of information collection, clinical inference, and forecasting under partial observability, often without a uniquely correct treatment decision. Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong medical question-answering capabilities but extending these advances to clinical reasoning remains challenging because many clinical tasks are only partially verifiable. Recent clinical reinforcement learning (RL) work has therefore largely focused on diagnostic endpoints, where correctness can be more directly verified. Oncology treatment decision making is a particularly challenging instance of this problem that extends beyond medical question-answering in which multiple decision trajectories can be clinically defensible for the same partially observed patient state. We formulate oncology treatment planning as an interactive RL problem under partial observability and construct patient-specific, process-aware rewards from decision processes observed in real world clinical records. The agent must learn to sequentially acquire information from a structured patient representation before committing to a first-line treatment recommendation, and is evaluated on information gathering, treatment selection, and reasoning alignment. Using GRPO and GDPO to optimize Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct within this environment, RL substantially improves the base policy beyond frontier model capabilities. The GDPO-trained policy increases Mean@3 scalar clinical reward by $79.60\%$ over base Llama and exceeds GPT-5.2 by $18.82\%$ under the same interactive environment, while reducing query count during training. Overall, our results show that patient-specific, partially verifiable rewards can provide an effective learning signal for interactive clinical RL when both the patient state and the underlying clinical decision process are only partially observed.

MedHarnessBench: A Unified Benchmark for Healthcare Agent Evaluation

Rui Tang, Hongqiu Wang

Healthcare agents must transform longitudinal clinical evidence into reliable actions through heterogeneous tools and stateful workflows, conditions under which model-centric systems may lose context, misuse tools, fail to verify consequential transitions, or cross authorization boundaries. Existing medical-agent benchmarks primarily emphasize terminal task outcomes, making it difficult to determine whether execution failures arise from the agent model, the runtime harness, or their interaction. We present the Medical Harness Protocol, an outcome-preserving framework that independently evaluates seven healthcare-specific process dimensions: Care Action Fulfillment, Clinical Resource Competence, Patient-Evidence Alignment, Clinical Workflow Continuity, Clinical Auditability, Closed-Loop Verification, and Clinical Conduct Boundaries. A normalized intervention trace further tracks whether runtime support is available, activated, delivered, adopted by the agent, and propagated into subsequent behavior. We instantiate the protocol as MedHarnessBench, comprising 342 executable tasks across FHIR/EHR operations, healthcare-administration interfaces, and multimodal clinical tools. Across more than 16,000 controlled task executions, MedHarness yields broad improvements in terminal task success, intermediate subtask completion, and healthcare-specific process quality. We further evaluate MedHarness against multiple general-purpose harnesses and use controlled single-fault perturbations to verify the sensitivity and discrimination of the seven-dimensional evaluation. These results demonstrate that healthcare-specific runtime support can substantially improve both task completion and execution quality, while revealing failures that terminal outcomes alone cannot capture. Code and resources are available at https://github.com/Tomry1114/Awesome-Agent-Harness-for-Healthcare.

ClinEdge: An Expert-Graded Benchmark of Real Patient Edge Cases Where Frontier Multimodal Models Fail

Anurag Sharma, Aditya Sharma, Sapna Baalwan

Most benchmarks for large language models in medicine are built from licensing examinations and theoretical questions. The few that draw on real clinical data do so only in part, and rarely require a model to read a patient's own imaging. ClinEdge addresses this gap with 32 real, de-identified patient cases from active clinical practice, each evaluated across seven frontier systems for a total of 224 expert-graded evaluations. Each case pairs primary imaging with a short vignette, contains a feature known to defeat pattern-matched reasoning, and is scored against the patient's documented clinical course. The suite is built for depth rather than volume. Grading followed a two-stage expert process. First, a senior orthopaedic surgeon graded all 224 responses on a weighted rubric and a pass-or-fail verdict. Second, a panel of nineteen physicians, largely senior specialists at teaching medical colleges, independently reviewed every case and every one of the 224 grades. All seven production systems were evaluated through their public interfaces, and all 224 conversations are preserved as replayable links. Performance was poor across all systems, and the failures were shared: different vendors missed the same findings and converged on the same wrong plans. From the experts' notes we catalogue 18 recurring failure modes in five families. The models stop reading an image at the first finding. They give generic plans and lean toward over-treatment. They trust a misleading history over correct imaging. Their answers vary across systems and runs, and change with question format. Failing answers were delivered fluently and without any expressed uncertainty. Four cases are released in full; the remaining 28 are held out, and we invite frontier AI labs and healthcare AI companies to evaluate their models against them (research@futrbridge.com)

This ability unlocks interoperability in the healthcare domain, allowing for extraction and alignment between different medical standards. From Hindsight to Foresight: Dissecting Clinical Evidence Acquisition in Large Language Models

Ziyang Ling

Large language models (LLMs) often diagnose full sight, static clinical cases well yet struggle in foresight, dynamic diagnosis. Prior work has improved this limitation by quantifying the information models acquire and by developing stronger follow-up strategies to improve diagnostic accuracy. In contrast, we introduce a distinctive hindsight to systematically dissect why LLMs fail to acquire diagnostically critical evidence, tracing the failure from evidence identification, utilization and downstream diagnostic updating through not only diagnostic accuracy, but also evidence coverage, trajectory dynamics, and elicited probability and information entropy. Starting experiments from the breakdown at the level of capabilities, to the diagnosis of the pre-event state before the evidence arrives, and to the post-event state transition after the evidence arrives, our research has revealed the profound limitations and internal causes existing in the dynamic diagnostic process. At the same time, we have also conducted sufficient verification and generalization experiments to prove that these limitations are widespread. Together, these findings shift the central challenge from whether LLMs possess relevant clinical knowledge to whether they can identify, acquire, and effectively use the right evidence under clinical diagnosis and conduct a more detailed mechanism analysis to identify the exact location of the capability gap.

Our framework achieved up to 99.8% ACC in identifying adversarial directional attacks, significantly outperforming existing defense mechanisms. It consistently identified adversarial samples across varying attack strengths while keeping false positives low, showing strong reliability and potential for clinical use. Furthermore, our proposal highlights its potential for real-world deployment.

We attribute this to the lack of a successful organizing principle that leads to generalizable conclusions about monitoring reliability in different regimes. Recent work attributes monitoring failures to the CoT not being necessary for the answer, and argues that CoT becomes monitorable when it is necessary. We show that this binary necessary/not-necessary organizing principle is too coarse by demonstrating that monitoring failures persist even if the CoT is necessary for the answer. On a difficult medical-diagnosis task, patient sex and a clinical attribute influence the answer in at least 19% and 24% of correct responses with a necessary CoT, respectively, yet their influence is almost never articulated. These results show that even when CoT is necessary, it is not guaranteed to be monitorable.

Automated disease classification from patient-reported symptoms requires balancing the speed of lightweight discriminative models against the contextual understanding and latency costs of autoregressive large language models (LLMs). Decision foundation models offer an alternative by mapping representations directly to candidate classes without free-form text generation. Using 4-bit quantized low-rank adaptation (QLoRA) with differential learning rates and a stratified split of 960 training and 240 test cases, the model achieves 95.42% diagnostic accuracy and a macro F1 score of 0.9551. Learning to Detect Shifts in Unstructured Data Streams via Monitoring-by-Betting

Siyu Zhou, Jialin Ouyang, Fan Xia, Adarsh Subbaswamy, Drew Prinster, Suchi Saria

Continuous monitoring of generative AI systems requires detecting distribution shifts in the unstructured data they ingest and generate. Existing monitoring methods typically control false alarms by requiring the discrepancy measure used to detect shifts to be *pre-specified*. However, for unstructured data, pre-specification creates a sensitivity--breadth trade-off: a narrowly defined measure is sensitive to the anticipated shift but may miss unexpected changes, whereas a broadly defined measure may detect a wider range of shifts but with reduced sensitivity. Although adaptively hypothesizing the shift direction can overcome this limitation, such data-dependent adaptation generally invalidates conventional false-alarm guarantees. We reconcile adaptivity with statistical validity through testing-by-betting, reframing sequential changepoint detection as a betting game in which the bettor continually updates its hypothesized shift direction, or witness function, as new observations arrive. We introduce *asymptotic e-detectors*, which approximate the pre-change distribution using a calibration set of size $m$, and prove that they asymptotically control the average run length to a false alarm as $m$ increases. Empirically, calibration sets as small as $m=30$ provide effective control. We then develop adaptive monitoring strategies ranging from black-box bettors that learn from embeddings to interpretable bettors based on LLM-proposed concepts. Across three real-world datasets, including data from a clinical AI scribe deployment, adaptive monitors detect shifts faster than fixed monitors at the same false-alarm level. Further, when the true shifts are sparse and interpretable, LLM-concept monitors provide the fastest and most interpretable detection, while also guiding root-cause diagnosis for complex shifts.
