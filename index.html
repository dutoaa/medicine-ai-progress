Interpretable Deep Learning Models for Breast Cancer Diagnosis Using Medical Imaging

Sami Alnawali, Muhammad Atif Saeed, Abdul Hadi, Azka Atiq, Akhtar Jamil, Ahmad Din

Deep learning shows promise for breast cancer diagnosis, but limited interpretability hinders clinical adoption. We benchmark ten ImageNet-pretrained models (ResNet50, VGG19, InceptionV3, DenseNet121, Xception, MobileNetV2, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt, ViT, and Swin Transformer) for benign-versus-malignant classification on the BreakHis histopathology dataset using a patient-level split, and explain their predictions with Grad-CAM, LIME, Integrated Gradients, and LRP. DenseNet121 performed best, with 99.4% test accuracy and an AUC-ROC of 0.999, and significantly outperformed all other models (p < 0.001). Transformer-based models trailed the CNNs. The explanations consistently highlighted nuclei-rich and glandular regions of diagnostic relevance. Combining transfer learning with explainable AI can thus yield accurate, interpretable tools for breast cancer diagnosis.

ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents

Sirry Chen, Fangke Chen, Xuanwen Ding, Wei Chen, Zhongyu Wei

Disease diagnosis is a key application of large language model (LLM) agents in healthcare. However, existing medical agents are primarily trained and evaluated with static question-answering data, providing limited opportunities to learn active information acquisition, examination decision-making, and dynamic clinical interaction. To address this limitation, we present ClinVerse, an interactive, real-data-grounded multi-agent environment designed for training and evaluating medical diagnostic agents. Built on ClinPatient, a scalable dataset comprising 150k diagnostic cases derived from real-world electronic health records, ClinVerse supports coordinated interactions among six specialized clinical roles. Specifically, a doctor agent starts from an initial patient complaint, progressively gathers clinical evidence through patient interviews and examination requests, and ultimately produces a diagnosis. For each interaction trajectory, a rule-based scoring framework quantifies information acquisition, examination ordering, and diagnostic accuracy. These trajectory-level scores serve as reward signals for agent training while also enabling scalable evaluation. In experiments, we evaluate various general-purpose and medical-domain models and show that nine self-evolution methods across three categories consistently improve agents' diagnostic capabilities within ClinVerse. We further analyze the interplay among model capability, environment feedback, and self-evolution methods.

MedJev: Evaluating Zero-Shot System-One Decision Making in Medicine

Simon A. Lee, Jiahang Sha, Moonseong Jeong, Yunbei Pan, Jeffrey N. Chiang

System One decision models such as Jev answer structured questions in a single forward pass without generating text, offering fast and computationally efficient clinical decision support. We evaluate MedJev, an open System One model that runs locally on a laptop, across ten medical benchmarks spanning medical knowledge, diagnosis, and patient level prediction using MIMIC IV and MIMIC IV ED. We compare MedJev against zero shot LLM baselines, published GPT results, and supervised models. MedJev achieves accuracies of 72.5 percent on MedQA, 75.4 percent on USMLE, and 80.1 percent on NEJM, approaching published GPT 4 performance while maintaining well calibrated confidence estimates (ECE 0.028 to 0.063). On patient level tasks, MedJev achieves an AUROC of 0.926 for In Hospital Mortality, exceeding XGBoost (0.902), but underperforms supervised models on operational outcomes such as ED Triage Admission (0.745 vs. 0.809). Patient level predictions also exhibit substantial miscalibration (In Hospital Mortality ECE 0.286). We introduce a lightweight, two parameter recalibration method that adapts predictions to local patient populations using 50 to 200 labeled examples, reducing ECE to 0.007. Our findings establish open System One models as a promising zero shot baseline for medical AI, combining competitive predictive performance with low computational cost and efficient local calibration. We release our code and evaluation harness to support future research.

Ontology-guided multi-agent reasoning for explainable differential diagnosis

Roxana Ramona Szomiu, George Ursu, Adrian Groza

Effective differential diagnosis depends on well structured and unambiguous patient case descriptions, since incomplete or ambiguous clinical information readily leads to diagnostic errors and inconsistent reasoning. Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently shown promising capabilities in medical question answering and clinical reasoning; however, even when achieving competitive performance on medical benchmarks, they often rely on pattern recognition learned from training data rather than grounded clinical evidence, and can generate plausible yet unsupported diagnostic conclusions. Their reasoning remains largely opaque, making it difficult for clinicians to assess the evidence and rationale behind a given recommendation. To address this gap, we propose an ontology-guided, multiagent differential diagnosis framework that replicates the iterative reasoning process of physicians. The framework grounds diagnostic inference in a structured, expert-curated medical knowledge base, enabling interpretation of symptoms, clinical findings, or disease relationships, while decomposing diagnosis into explicit, auditable reasoning steps. This ontology-based grounding constrains inference to evidence-backed associations, reducing hallucination and improving diagnostic transparency, traceability, and consistency. The system then identifies the most discriminative missing symptoms and generates targeted follow-up questions to reduce uncertainty through a process that mirrors to clinical inquiry. Together, these components improve the reliability, explainability, and diagnostic accuracy of AI-assisted differential diagnosis. The source code is publicly available.

Cholesterol Paradox: Multi-point Heart Failure Prediction from Lipid Panels in Electronic Health Records

Hou, Y.; Bhuiyan, M. K.; Samad, M.

In cardiovascular medicine, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is the main lipid marker used in routine screening to assess cardiovascular risk and guide preventive treatment and dietary changes. An increasing number of studies have recently documented the cholesterol paradox: patients with or at risk of heart failure (HF) often have unexpectedly low levels of LDL-C despite leading to cardiovascular diseases. This paper examines how well retrospective lipid panel measurements, supplemented with apolipoprotein B (ApoB), can predict an initial heart failure (HF) event in several pre-diagnosis time windows, using age-matched patient data from Electronic Health Records (EHRs). This retrospective study uses lipid profiles from multicenter nationwide EHRs from the All of Us Research Program. We apply machine learning and explainable AI (SHAP) to lipid-based prediction and to interpret cholesterol contributions to the diagnosis of first heart failure (HF). One experiment predicts the diagnosis of HF in 36 months; the other distinguishes HF patients from people who have never been diagnosed with cardiovascular disease. A baseline lipid panel that includes ApoB predicts the first HF event up to 36 months in advance, achieving an AUC of 0.624 in men and 0.627 in women. Compared to a group of the same age without previous cardiovascular disease, a lipid profile including ApoB predicts a first HF event with a substantially higher AUC: 0.937 for men and 0.925 for women. Shapley value analyses suggest that higher triglycerides and lower HDL and LDL levels are linked to subsequent HF events, with LDL appearing as the strongest yet counterintuitive predictor. ApoB ranks second in importance; in males, higher ApoB is associated with a greater risk of HF, unlike LDL. In general, the study highlights an unexpected association with LDL and emphasizes the prognostic utility of sex-specific metabolic indicators (TG, HDL) for HF using routinely collected clinical data.

In this Perspective, we argue for a fundamental shift from data aggregation to experience aggregation. We propose Decentralized Agentic Systems (DAS), a framework in which distributed AI agents operating across healthcare institutions synthesize collective clinical intelligence by sharing abstracted reasoning trajectories rather than raw data or model parameters. We examine the architectural principles, deployment considerations, governance requirements, and critical open challenges of this framework, laying the groundwork for a collaborative medical AI ecosystem where distributed expertise autonomously adapts to the complexities of longitudinal patient care.

OA-RAG: Ontology-based Agentic RAG for Faithful LLM Reasoning

Hui Yang, Jiaoyan Chen, Yongsheng Gao

Large language models (LLMs) perform well across many real-world tasks but remain prone to hallucination, limiting their reliability in domains such as biomedicine and healthcare, where answers must be grounded in established knowledge. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) mitigates this problem by incorporating external information, but often overlooks logical relationships and relies heavily on the LLM's own reasoning, which can compromise answer faithfulness. We propose OA-RAG, an agent that integrates formal reasoning with LLM reasoning, drawing on both the logical structure and informal annotations of domain ontologies. For each answer, OA-RAG reports the ontology observations used as evidence and a model-assessed grounding level (entailed, supported, related, or unrelated) that characterises the strength of that evidence. Across benchmarks derived from three large-scale ontologies and six real-world medical question-answering tasks, OA-RAG achieves the best overall performance among the evaluated ontology-fine-tuned and popular RAG methods, with particularly strong results on reasoning-intensive tasks. The reported grounding levels also offer a useful indication of answer reliability, with stronger grounding generally associated with higher accuracy. Finally, our results demonstrate the value of ontologies as knowledge sources: OA-RAG is competitive with, and sometimes outperforms, domain-specific RAG systems that draw on sources such as textbooks.
