Medical Specialty Prediction from Bangla Patient Narratives: A Comparative Study of ML, DL, Transformers, and LLMs

Tasnin Tabassum Ali, Faisal Ahmed, Muhammad Anwarul Azim

Timely access to appropriate medical specialists remains a bottleneck in Bangladesh's healthcare system, as patients describe symptoms in informal Bangla rather than clinical terms, leading to ineffective referrals. This paper compares four model families for predicting medical specialties from Bangla patient narratives: six machine learning (ML) models using hybrid TF-IDF features, four deep learning (DL) models using Word2Vec embeddings trained on the corpus, four fine-tuned transformers, and five large language models (LLMs) evaluated zero-shot. From a corpus of 3798 patient records covering 20 specialties, we retained the five with the most records (Medicine, Gynecology, Dermatology, Pediatrics, Gastroenterology) and split the resulting 2730 records 80:20 into training and test sets shared by all families. The best transformer was XLM-RoBERTa (86.26% accuracy, 86.21% macro-F1), the best ML model was SVM (84.98%, 84.92%), the best DL model was Bidirectional GRU (80.77%, 80.83%), and the best LLM was Gemini (90.29%, 90.15%). Holm-corrected paired McNemar tests show SVM significantly outperforming Bi-LSTM (adjusted p = 0.004), whereas the Gemini-versus-Grok gap does not survive correction (adjusted p = 0.104), and neither the strongest transformer versus the weakest LLM (Grok) nor SVM versus the strongest transformer reaches significance. A tuned classical model is thus statistically indistinguishable from the strongest transformer, so accuracy rankings alone should not be read as evidence of one family's superiority. The findings provide a comparative baseline for Bangla medical text classification.

InterGenEHR$^+$: An Interactive Framework for Natural Language-Conditioned Generation of Longitudinal Patient Trajectories

Yu Yang, Jiafeng Song, Baraa F Abed, Christina Creel, Sagar B Dave, Henry Foote

Objective: To develop and validate InterGenEHR$^+$, an interactive framework for natural language-conditioned generation of synthetic tabular electronic health records (EHRs) and longitudinal trajectories for scenario-based evaluation of machine learning models. Methods: An optional large language model (LLM) parser converts clinical requests into structured constraints. Conditional variational autoencoders and diffusion models generate single-time-point records, while diffusion models generate longitudinal trajectories. We evaluate parsing, distributional fidelity, imputation, clinical-constraint adherence, mortality prediction, and clinical plausibility using a multi-institutional cohort of 874 patients receiving venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO). Results: Gemma 3 4B with few-shot prompting achieves the highest parsing performance with a low spurious slot rate. IGE-TabDDPM and IGE-PMVAE perform well in single-time-point generation; IGE-CSDI, IGE-FGTI, and IGE-MTSCI lead on different longitudinal fidelity metrics. Across five random seeds and three classifiers, synthetic cohorts retain predictive utility for mortality prediction on real test data. Clinician ratings support trajectory plausibility and alignment with requested scenarios. Conclusion: InterGenEHR$^+$ supports clinician-specified generation of EHR trajectories with statistical fidelity and downstream predictive utility in the evaluated VV-ECMO cohort. Extension to other clinical settings requires further validation. Significance: The framework supports scenario-based evaluation of machine learning models in data-sparse critical care settings.

JHealthBench-L: Clinician-Reviewed Localization of HealthBench for Rubric-Based Medical LLM Evaluation in Japan

Shohei Hisada, Keisuke Orimo, Kenji Igarashi, Kota Sato, Masahiro Kazama, Mitsuhisa Ota

Rubric-based benchmarks such as HealthBench evaluate large language models (LLMs) on open-ended health consultations using case-specific physician criteria, many of which presuppose the healthcare institutions, medications, and care pathways of their source region. Under machine translation alone, these regional assumptions remain in the rubric, leaving responses appropriate in Japan uncredited or falsely penalized under foreign clinical rules. To adapt established clinical evaluation perspectives to Japan without authoring rubrics from scratch, we construct JHealthBench-L (79 patient-facing consultations, 1,022 criteria) through source-cited LLM drafting, single-specialist selection and revision, independent dual-physician review, and meta-review. Specialists rewrote only 4.4% of criteria from scratch, and independent reviewers approved 93.6% of Stage-2 criteria without issue. Comparing JHealthBench-L with a machine-translated baseline across 18 LLMs reveals that translation-only evaluation preserves coarse model rankings ($τ= 0.817$, mean shift +0.027) yet flips verdicts on 17.7% of modified criteria (vs. 7.3% re-grading noise) and reduces false penalties on Japan-adapted negative criteria by 7.1 percentage points, masking item-level clinical mismeasurement behind stable leaderboards. Axis-level and paired analyses further show that context awareness and Japan-specific clinical criteria remain primary bottlenecks, whereas Japanese continual pretraining and reasoning post-training substantially improve locally grounded performance. Datasets, physician revision logs, and evaluation code are available at https://github.com/horshohei/JHealthBench-L.

Ying Jiang, Juntao Jiang, Linxuan Fan, Lingling Li, Yaqi Wang, Xiuzhen Yang

Background: Testicular appendage torsion (TAT) is the leading cause of acute scrotal pain in prepubertal children. Yet, its diagnosis remains challenging due to nonspecific clinical manifestations and the absence of standardized datasets for pediatric acute scrotal emergencies. Results: We present TAT-Det, an expert-annotated ultrasound dataset specifically designed for TAT. TAT-Det comprises 2,600 ultrasound images from 1,203 patients collected at the Children's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Each TAT-positive image is accompanied by lesion-level bounding boxes for detection and pixel-level segmentation masks for fine-grained delineation. Baseline benchmarks for object detection and semantic segmentation using representative deep learning models are further provided, with YOLO11m achieving the best detection performance and RWKV-UNet the best segmentation performance. Conclusions: TAT-Det addresses the lack of publicly available resources for pediatric emergency ultrasound and establishes a foundation for developing and evaluating artificial intelligence methods for automated TAT analysis.

Mitigating Hallucinations in Medical Question Answering on ESC Cardiology Guidelines: Evaluation and Retrieval Optimization

Ilkin Masimov, Rufana Baghirli, Muhammad Rashid

Background: Large language models produce hallucinations in medical question answering (QA). Verify-and-revise pipelines that check atomic claims against retrieved guideline evidence are a standard mitigation, but evaluations almost universally treat the verifier's output as ground truth, an assumption rarely tested. Objective: To measure, claim by claim against human judgement, how such a pipeline fails in a guideline-grounded clinical setting. Methods: We built a dataset of 160 questions (100 patient vignettes from 89 de-identified cases, 60 fact-based) over four recent ESC cardiology guidelines, all approved by a practising internist, and a retrieval corpus of 627 hand-verified guideline records at two chunk granularities. A five-stage pipeline was run under four retrieval configurations and three independent runs (1920 arm-runs); all 606 first-pass refuted claims were manually audited, an independent judge re-judged 906 decisions, and the expert blindly reviewed 100 accepted claims. Results: Only 44.7-50.6% of flagged claims were true hallucinations. Correction loops converged in 85.2-95.1% of cases, yet 35% of genuine final errors were introduced by correction. Single-run true-hallucination recall was 35.6-41.2%, the three-run union 71-80%; verifier agreement was moderate (kappa = 0.505). The blind probe estimated the accepted-claim miss rate at 3.2% (95% CI 0.7-6.7%). Final genuine-error rate: 1.7% of answers. Conclusion: Claim-level verification reduces hallucinated content by an order of magnitude, but verifier reliability, not generation, is the limiting factor and must be measured, not assumed.

EPPC-CARE: Staged Hierarchical Annotation of Communication Behaviors in Secure Patient-Provider Messages

Samah Fodeh, Sreeraj Ramachandran, Ganesh Puthiaraju, Sarah Schellhorn

Objectives: Patient portal messages provide invaluable information about communication dynamics between patients and healthcare providers, but extracting it manually is labor-intensive. We developed and evaluated EPPC-CARE, an automated framework for identifying communication behaviors together with the supporting text for each classification. Materials and Methods: From 867 messages, we obtained 5,516 reference standard annotations. Each annotation is a triplet of communication-related text (span), a broader communication category (Code), and a specific subcategory (Sub-code). We evaluated EPPC-CARE's identified triplets across six open-weight large language models (LLMs) against supervised fine-tuning and communication-based models such as EPPC-OASIS. We also compared EPPC-CARE with human expert annotations on 100 messages. Results: EPPC-CARE improved performance over EPPC-OASIS across all six models. EPPC-CARE's Triplet F1 improved by 2.90 percentage points on average, with a best score of 64.4%. On the human expert evaluation subset, EPPC-CARE and the human expert showed comparable agreement with the reference standard, with differences favoring EPPC-CARE. Discussion: These findings suggest that identifying triplet components (span, code, subcode) sequentially can improve performance across model families while preserving the supporting text behind each identification. EPPC-CARE links the communication code and subcode with the source text (span), supporting human verification of automated annotations in clinical informatics. EPPC-CARE may therefore make larger-scale studies of patient-provider communication more feasible and provide a foundation for human-in-the-loop annotation workflows. Conclusion: EPPC-CARE offers a more reliable and reviewable approach for automated characterization of communication behaviors in secure patient-provider messaging.

Can LLM Judges Catch Unsafe Clinical Actions? A Fault-Injection Study

Neda Ghafouri

As clinical AI agents begin to interact with electronic health records (EHRs), large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to evaluate their actions. But how reliably can these judges recognize clinically dangerous mistakes, especially when an agent appears to have completed its task successfully? We investigate this question using MedAgentBench by introducing controlled clinical errors into verified agent trajectories. The experiments include 416 faulty trajectories, eight error categories, and six open-weight LLM judges. By comparing each faulty trajectory with its correct counterpart, we measure whether judges genuinely recognize errors rather than simply flagging more actions as unsafe. The findings show that access to clinical evidence significantly improves judge reliability. When shown only the final answer, judges cannot distinguish errors that leave it unchanged, while flagging 56% of correct trajectories. With full trajectory information, the ability to distinguish unsafe actions from correct ones reaches a score of 0.75, and providing actual EHR responses improves discrimination by 0.39. Surprisingly, including the agent's reasoning does not improve overall performance and reduces detection of incorrectly reported clinical values by 0.18, which remain the hardest errors to detect. MedGemma performed worse than the general-purpose model and was the only judge to miss potentially dangerous medication overdoses. We also find that the benchmark's original grader accepts all 38 trajectories with an intentionally skipped laboratory check, while combining LLM judges with a rule-based checker informed by the full EHR record detects 97% of injected faults.

Laya-IsoToken: Day-One Clinical Codebook Switching via Closed-Form Operator Compilation

Zeqiang Wang

Healthcare systems periodically revise diagnostic and procedural classifications (ICD-9 to ICD-10/11), update hospital reimbursement groupers (MS-DRG), and deploy clinical coding across languages. Supervised coding networks bind clinical text encoders to fixed per-code classifier weights, forcing hospitals to re-annotate tens of thousands of records before switching codebooks, whereas standalone large language models incur high latency and hallucinate invalid codes. Here we show that day-one clinical codebook switching becomes feasible without target-labeled notes when local clinical evidence reading is decoupled from a strictly frozen multilingual concept space. Laya-IsoToken trains a clinical span reader once on source records with zero per-code classifier parameters, separating text evidence from historical disease prevalence. Given only public target code descriptions, closed-form matrix compilation aligns distributional shifts, compensates for paraphrased definitions, and deduplicates sibling codes in under three seconds, while a pre-deployment geometric residual routes unfamiliar concepts to offline language model verification. Across 32,804 MIMIC-IV ICD-9 discharges, Laya-IsoToken matches published supervised baselines (62.6% Micro-F1) while reducing active calibration error by 32%. Switched with zero target training notes to 13,783 unseen-patient ICD-10 discharges, MS-DRG groups, and multilingual cohorts, it outperforms biomedical dense retrievers, semantic crosswalks (+5.4 F1 points on ICD-10, paired $d_z = 0.49$, $p < 0.001$), and official US CMS equivalence mappings (+3.5 points, $p < 0.001$) at 9.4 ms per admission. Pairing this reader with an offline 31B verifier across 1,000 full discharge summaries lifts zero-training ICD-10 coding to 40.6% Micro-F1 (57.0% category F1, $d_z = 0.66$, $p < 0.001$) with 0.0% invalid code hallucinations.

The ITE-Transformer: A pre-trained foundation model for predicting individualized treatment effects from electronic health records

Arceri, C.; Angelis, E.; Heesen, P.; Melnychuk, V.; Bendszus, M.; Bauer, S.; Feuerriegel, S.

Transformer-based foundation models have shown strong potential for modeling longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs), yet existing models focus primarily on risk prediction rather than treatment effect estimation, which limits evidence generation. Here, we introduce the ITE-Transformer, a transformer-based framework that combines self-supervised representation learning from longitudinal patient trajectories with doubly robust causal machine learning for predicting individualized treatment effects (ITEs). As a result, our ITE-Transformer allows to study counterfactual questions of how an individual patients outcome may differ under alternative treatment strategies. The ITE-Transformer learns temporally contextualized representations of patients diagnosis and medication histories and subsequently uses these representations to predict treatment assignment, treatment-specific outcome risks, and patient-specific ITEs. The ITE-Transformer is trained using structured EHR data from[~] 350,000 participants in the All of Us Research Program and evaluated in two clinically different comparative-effectiveness tasks: (i) GLP-1 receptor agonists versus DPP-4 inhibitors among patients with type 2 diabetes, with major adverse cardiovascular events as the outcome, and (ii) immunotherapy versus chemotherapy in a pan-cancer cohort, with all-cause mortality as the outcome. Across both applications, the ITE-Transformer improves the discrimination and calibration of treatment assignment and outcome models compared with existing tabular machine learning approaches and an otherwise identical transformer trained without self-supervised pre-training. The ITE-Transformer further identifies substantial heterogeneity in predicted ITEs and yields interpretable patterns of treatment response associated with patients diagnoses, medication histories, and comorbidities. Together, our work demonstrates how clinical foundation models can be developed for analyzing heterogeneity in treatment effects and thus support real-world evidence generation.

ASCENT: A Benchmark for Evaluating and Advancing Stepwise Diagnostic Reasoning in Large Language Models on Common Clinical Scenarios

Yera Choi, Yeong Hwa Kim, Jaedeok Lee, Taekang Kim, Sangdoo Yun, Seong-Eun Moon

Large language models (LLMs) excel at medical question answering yet are rarely evaluated on the stepwise diagnostic reasoning that defines real clinical workflows, where impressions are revised as information accumulates. We build Annotated Stepwise Clinical rEasoning for NaturalisTic Diagnosis (ASCENT), a clinician-annotated benchmark and training resource of 3,078 stepwise problems derived from MedQA-USMLE that decomposes each vignette into EMR-aligned steps (Findings, Impression, supporting Rationale), enabling evaluation of intermediate reasoning under incomplete information. Experiments and training with ASCENT revealed insights into how current LLMs handle stepwise diagnostic reasoning. Even strong reasoning models that perform well on MedQA-USMLE leave substantial headroom on ASCENT, and general-purpose frontier models trail further, exposing a persistent gap between fully informed and stepwise diagnosis. Fine-tuning Qwen2.5-7B and 32B on ASCENT yields measurable F1 gains over both pre-trained and HuatuoGPT-o1 CoT-trained baselines, with gains driven primarily by precision. Complementary robustness analyses (counterfactual perturbation, format-vs-content control, judge agreement, and rollout) further show that ASCENT-fine-tuned models rely on the diagnostic content of prior impressions rather than imitating their output format, while error propagation under rollout remains a key challenge for clinical deployment.

A dataset of clinically similar image pairs from citation-linked case reports for medical image retrieval

Guy Shmueli, Keno K. Bressem, Tom Hope

Retrieving images of patients clinically similar to a given patient can support diagnosis, teaching and research. Training and evaluating retrieval systems requires knowing which images of different patients show the same finding, and existing datasets are small or narrow in scope. We construct PMC-PaIR, a dataset of 86,144 pairs of clinically similar images from 56,013 open-access PubMed Central case reports, which often document rare or challenging presentations. We develop an approach that follows citations, which often link case reports of similar patients. To determine what two linked cases share clinically, it prompts large language models to generate and answer hierarchical questions, from modality to diagnosis and findings, and to match images by caption using the answers. Experts find the pairs highly accurate, and this question-guided approach finds twice as many correct pairs as direct matching. Fine-tuning BiomedCLIP on PMC-PaIR improves retrieval in all modality, anatomy and diagnosis categories tested, for image-only queries and image queries with a short text naming the finding. We release the dataset and the automated pipeline, which can extend PMC-PaIR as new case reports are published.

Sage-Med: Scaling Medical Multimodal Intelligence

Sage-Med Team, :, Ming Hu, Cheng Tang, Wei Li, Rongbin Li

Establishing effective scaling laws for medical multimodal large language models (MLLMs) remains a persistent and fundamental challenge. Our systematic evaluation shows that existing medical MLLMs tend to underperform frontier general-purpose MLLMs across a range of medical tasks, while scaling model size primarily improves text capabilities rather than multimodal perception, and enabling reasoning modes fails to close this visual gap. To investigate how medical multimodal intelligence scales in practice, we introduce Sage-Med-Preview, a 35B-parameter medical multimodal model with 3B activated parameters, built by combining stage-specific curation over 322.7 million candidate records with a progressive training curriculum spanning medical pretraining, staged supervised fine-tuning, and adaptive reinforcement learning. Although substantial challenges remain in complex medical reasoning and multi-step clinical workflows, this report provides empirical evidence, actionable data and training recipes, and a rigorous benchmark toward scalable clinical multimodal intelligence.

BayesPath: Reliability-Aware Bayesian Reasoning for Whole-Slide Pathology Agents

Bryan Wong, Xun Xu, Jongwoo Kim, Huazhu Fu, Nancy F. Chen, Mun Yong Yi

Diagnosing whole-slide images (WSIs) requires hypothesis-driven reasoning: pathologists form a differential diagnosis, seek discriminative evidence, weigh its reliability, and stop once it suffices. However, existing training-free WSI agents lack an explicit framework for such reasoning. They typically use a large language model (LLM) to aggregate the region descriptions of a multimodal LLM (MLLM), without a belief over the candidates or regard for the descriptions' reliability. Consequently, a repeated model error counts as independent evidence for an incorrect diagnosis. We introduce BayesPath, a training-free WSI agent that casts the pathologist's Scan-Look-Weigh-Decide process as reliability-aware Bayesian reasoning. An explicit belief over the candidates is updated with each observation through a reliability-aware likelihood and guides where to look next and when to stop. This likelihood corrects for the models' answer bias, discounts correlated observations, and captures diagnostic confusions estimated without labels.
