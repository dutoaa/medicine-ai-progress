Developing an OMOP-Standardized Prostate Cancer Database and Improving Data Quality Using NLP and PSA-Based Algorithms

Wang, J.; Jackson, J. C.; Garza, A.; Nalla, S.; Ninnemann, T.; Zhang, Y.; Kuo, Y.-F.

ObjectiveTo develop and evaluate an Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership-(OMOP-) standardized prostate cancer database from the University of Texas Medical Branch (UTMB) Epic Electronic Health Record (EHR) and improve data quality using natural language processing(NLP) and prostate-specific antigen-(PSA) based algorithms. Materials and MethodsWe built a data pipeline to transform UTMB Epic EHR data for patients identified through the Galveston Cancer Registry (2010-2021) into OMOP Common Data Model (CDM) v5.4. Data quality was assessed by comparing the OMOP-standardized data with Galveston Cancer Registry data using availability agreement, Cohens kappa and Intraclass Correlation Coefficient. NLP was used to extract PSA, Gleason score, and cancer stage from clinical text, and PSA-based algorithms were used to identify missing treatment and biochemical recurrence. ResultsWe extracted 815 analytic cases from UTMB EHR. Among them, 700 (85.9%) were complete and concordant with the cancer registry. PSA showed excellent value agreement. Structured Gleason score and stage data were sparse (n<20), but NLP greately improved capture. Treatment agreement was good compared with the cancer registry and improved slightly for radical prostatectomy after applying a PSA-based algorithm. Using PSA trajectories, we identified 60 cases of biochemical recurrence. DiscussionThe OMOP-standardized data from UTMB showed good agreement with the cancer registry. However, structured EHR fields incompletely captured diagnosis, pathology, and treatment details. NLP and PSA-based algorithms substantially improved data capture. Manual review also revealed errors in registry data, showing that OMOP-standardized EHR data can complement and help improve cancer registry quality. Conclusion: OMOP standardization combined with NLP and PSA-based algorithms improved prostate cancer data quality and research readiness. Lay SummaryElectronic health record (EHR) systems contain valuable long-term health information about cancer, but their use is limited by difficulties in data extraction, the lack of standard data formats, and missing information in structured fields. In this study, we built a data pipeline to convert EHR data into the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model, a widely used standard format that facilitates data sharing and collaborative research. To address missing data, we used natural language processing (NLP, a computational technique that enables computers to interpret human language) to extract prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels, Gleason scores (a measure of cancer aggressiveness), and cancer stages from unstructured clinical notes. We also applied algorithms that track PSA blood trends over time to identify unrecorded cancer treatments and detect cancer recurrence. Comparing this database with a cancer registry demonstrated that standardized EHR records provide accurate clinical information and long-term follow-up. While structured EHR fields lacked key tumor characteristics, NLP dramatically improved their capture. Tracking PSA trends identified previously unrecorded treatments and flagged 60 potential cancer recurrences. Overall, combining standardized EHR formats with NLP and PSA-based algorithms creates a rich database to support real-world prostate cancer research.

From examinations to consultations: a physician-reviewed Japanese benchmark for large language models in traditional Kampo medicine

Takata, H.; Harada, Y.; Inoue, H.; Uneda, K.; Yoshino, T.; Nogami, T.

ObjectivesPatients and clinicians increasingly ask large language models (LLMs) about Kampo, Japans traditional medicine. Yet evaluations of LLMs have measured accuracy on examination questions, which ask what a model knows; a consultation asks whether it can apply that knowledge to the patient at hand. No benchmark had measured that. Materials and MethodsKampoBench comprises 74 consultation scenarios and 310 conversation-specific rubric criteria, following the design of HealthBench. Scenarios, criteria and reference answers were drafted by an LLM and revised by board-certified Kampo physicians. Ten current models from three vendors were evaluated. Because LLM graders are known to favor their own family, each response was graded by a model from each vendor. ResultsThe best model reached 74.8-86.3% across graders, the physician-revised reference answers 87.9-92.4%. Rankings were stable across graders (Spearmans rho 0.952-0.976), and the family-favoring deviation was 0.4 to 0.6 percentage points, below the bootstrap standard deviation of one measurement; grader leniency varied by 14.3 points. Models were most reliable on criteria about advising that care be sought (83.6%) and least reliable on those requiring a known crude-drug risk to be tied to the case (17.5%), though 79.2% of those responses named the drug. DiscussionThe shortfall lay in applying knowledge to the case rather than in holding it -- a distinction examination-format evaluations cannot make. ConclusionEvaluation in traditional medicine should measure the judgments a consultation requires, not recall alone. Quoted alongside the grader that produced it, a KampoBench score is a usable measure of those judgments. Lay summaryPeople increasingly ask artificial intelligence chatbots about Kampo, the traditional herbal medicine used alongside conventional care in Japan. Earlier tests used examination questions, which check what a chatbot knows. A real consultation asks something harder: whether the chatbot can apply that knowledge to the person asking. We built KampoBench, 74 realistic consultations in Japanese. For each one, specialist Kampo physicians reviewed a list of what a good answer should and should not contain, 310 items in all. We then tested ten current chatbots from three companies. The answers were scored by chatbots, and a chatbot might favor its own companys chatbots, so every answer was scored by one chatbot from each company. No such favoritism was detected, but the scorers differed in strictness. The best chatbot scored 75-86%, depending on the scorer; answers revised by physicians scored 88-92%. The chatbots were good at telling people to seek medical care. They were poor at relating a known risk of an herbal ingredient to the persons situation, such as noticing that two medicines the person already takes contain the same ingredient, often even when they named it. Tests of chatbots in traditional medicine should measure such judgments, not knowledge alone.

Decision models can offload structured clinical tasks from large language models

Ariel, D.; Laban, G.; Endelman, S.; Balicer, R.; Dagan, N.; Cohen, S.

Decision models like Jev read text and return probabilities, categories, or ordinal levels, with confidence scores for the latter two, at a fraction of large language models (LLMs) cost. We explored how Jev could be integrated into clinical workflows for all these output types: mortality risk estimation, categorical Choosing-Wisely decisions for patient questions, and ordinal colon cancer staging. With standard LLM-style inputs, Jev trailed five flagship LLMs: mortality AUROC was 0.822 versus 0.837-0.846; Choosing-Wisely accuracy, 87.7% versus 98.4-100%; and staging accuracy, 45.4% versus 94.2-100%. Developer-recommended input alterations improved staging accuracy to 75.8% with medical context, 82.1% with task decomposition, and 85.8% with both. None of Jevs staging errors had full confidence. Using Jev for fully confident staging decisions and LLMs for the rest was non-inferior to each LLM alone and cut flagship LLM costs by 33-44%. Prompted as designed, decision models may efficiently share structured clinical tasks with LLMs.

Diagnostic accuracy and clinician-rated reasoning quality dissociate in a blinded evaluation of large language models in psychiatry

Jin, K. W.; Rostam-Abadi, Y.; Chaudhary, P.; Garrett, M. A.; Huang, A. S.; Montelongo, M.; Nagpal, C.; Shei, J.; Weathers, J.; Zhang, J. S.; Sharma, M. S.; Chen, Q.; Kim, J.; Malgaroli, M.; Mathis, W. S.; Rodriguez, C. I.; Selek, S.; Pittenger, C.; Yip, S. W.; Zaboski, B. A.; Xu, H.

BackgroundEvaluations of large language models (LLMs) in medicine rest largely on diagnostic accuracy. We report what we learned evaluating four frontier LLMs on psychiatric diagnosis, where we asked whether clinician assessment of model reasoning adds information that accuracy does not. MethodsFour LLMs produced ranked five-diagnosis differentials for 196 psychiatric vignettes (135 screened published case reports; 61 unpublished, clinician-authored). An automated adjudicator scored accuracy. On a random 30-vignette subset, five psychiatrists blinded to model identity judged correctness and rated reasoning on a 0-4 rubric adapted from the ACGME Psychiatry Milestones. We validated the adjudicator against the clinicians, measured the reproducibility of accuracy across generation runs and repeated adjudication, and tested whether computable reasoning trace features could substitute for clinician ratings. ResultsOn all 196 vignettes, automated top-5 accuracy ranged from 0.759 to 0.837, and the majority verdict of three adjudication passes separated one of six model pairs. On the 30 rated vignettes, clinician-determined accuracy separated no pair, while reasoning separated five. Two systems were equally accurate (0.827 each; difference bounded within {+/-}0.059, or {+/-}0.10 after Holm adjustment) yet differed by 0.70 reasoning points (95% CI, 0.50 to 0.90). The adjudicator agreed with held-out clinician majorities less closely than individual clinicians did (0.83 to 0.85 vs 0.89 to 0.96), regeneration changed top-1 correctness for 18 of 119 pairs, and reference strings that combined several diagnoses were scored as misses until split. Trace features that tracked ratings across model averages did not track them across individual traces. ConclusionsAccuracy did not distinguish models that clinicians rated differently, and automated accuracy depended on how it was scored. Where LLMs are meant to support clinical reasoning, structured clinician assessment of reasoning belongs in pre-deployment evaluation.

AI chatbots abandon sleep apnoea referral when simulated patients minimise symptoms

Ratneswaran, D.; Poirier, S.; Hamman, D.; Rykov, M.; Steier, J.

BackgroundPatients increasingly consult free artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots about their symptoms, but these models may favour agreement with the patient over accuracy. In obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), where most cases in the general population remain undiagnosed and diagnosis depends on referral, this could validate symptom denial. We tested whether consumer chatbots maintain guideline-concordant referral when a simulated patient minimises symptoms. MethodsSeven guideline-concordant and standardised OSA vignettes (apnoea-hypopnoea-index, AHI 8-52 events/h; body mass index 28-42 kg/m2) were presented to five free chatbots (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.6, DeepSeek V4 Flash and Grok-4.3) under neutral and symptom-minimising conditions (10 simulated conversations per model- vignette-condition cell; N=700). Referral maintenance was scored by a structured rubric and every capitulation was human-confirmed. ResultsAll chatbots maintained referral recommendations in neutral conversations (350/350; 100%). When simulated patients minimised symptoms and resisted referral, maintenance fell to 64.3% (225/350), a reduction of 35.7 percentage points (95% CI 5.5- 66.0; p=0.028). Rates ranged from 55.7% to 85.7% across chatbots, but evidence of between-chatbot differences was inconsistent across analyses. Rates differed across clinical vignettes (p=0.001). A variance analysis estimated greater variation between vignettes than between chatbots. In the three driving-risk vignettes, referral recommendations were abandoned in 46- 68% of symptom-minimising conversations, pooling all five chatbots within each vignette. ConclusionsConsumer AI chatbots abandoned correct OSA referral recommendations in about one-third of symptom-minimising conversations, with higher observed abandonment in simulated cases involving driving risk. This may delay appropriate assessment and raises patient- and public-safety concerns.

ClinStigma: A Clinician-Guided Framework for Developing Synthetic Benchmarks of Contextual Stigmatizing Language in Clinical Documentation

Izzo, J. A.; McIntyre, A. M.; Nguyen, J.; Bashaw, D.; Torrance, C. A.; Foster, J.

ObjectiveLarge language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to generate, summarize, and evaluate clinical documentation, yet standardized methods for evaluating nuanced, context-dependent clinical language remain limited. Existing approaches rely primarily on annotated clinical corpora or keyword-based methods, restricting reproducibility, public dissemination, and systematic comparison across models. We present ClinStigma, a clinician-guided framework for constructing, annotating, and evaluating synthetic benchmarks of contextual stigmatizing language, demonstrated through ClinStigma-SUD, its inaugural implementation for substance use disorder documentation. Materials and MethodsCandidate clinical sentences were generated using a frontier language model and iteratively reviewed according to a clinician-developed annotation rubric refined through expert review in stigmatizing language. The resulting benchmark intentionally incorporates explicit stigmatizing, neutral, and contextually ambiguous ("gray-zone") documentation requiring clinical reasoning rather than keyword recognition. Three locally deployed open-weight LLMs were evaluated using zero-shot inference, followed by external evaluation using independently annotated clinical documentation from MIMIC-IV. ResultsClinStigma-SUD consists of 500 clinician-adjudicated synthetic clinical documentation sentences. Inter-rater agreement was 98.8% across the full benchmark (Cohens {kappa} = 0.976) and 94.0% within the intentionally ambiguous subset ({kappa} = 0.880). Under zero-shot prompting, binary accuracy ranged from 84.8% to 91.8% across evaluated models, while F1 for the ambiguous (gray) class ranged from 0.038 to 0.423. Zero-shot external evaluation on 3,716 annotated examples from the independently annotated Harrigian MIMIC-IV corpus yielded 84.4% accuracy (84.4% balanced accuracy; macro F1 0.824), against a 69.9% majority-class baseline. Stigmatizing-class F1 was 0.879 in the substance-related subgroup and 0.883 in the full external corpus. Discussion and ConclusionClinStigma establishes a reproducible clinician-guided framework for developing synthetic evaluation benchmarks for contextual clinical language. By combining structured clinical expertise, explicit annotation guidance, synthetic benchmark generation, and external evaluation, the framework enables open, privacy-preserving evaluation of language models while providing a scalable foundation for benchmark development across additional clinical specialties, including obstetrics, emergency medicine, and mental health.

A Zero-Shot Decision Model (Jev) Matches a Trained Classifier but Ranks Its Own Errors Worse: Calibrated, Split-Conformal Next-Procedure Recommendation in General Dentistry

Rodrigues, C. C.; Rebello, S. D.

On real-world dental records, procedure history predicts the next procedure category well, and a simple history-aware classical model beats every large-language-model (LLM) design we tested. We study the question that follows every dental diagnosis: given a patients chart and most recent procedure, what should the dentist do next? Commercial dental artificial intelligence in 2026 is overwhelmingly diagnostic, and the closest published system for this sequencing task, MultiTP (Chen et al., 2024b), offers neither calibrated uncertainty, structured rationale, nor an evaluation as decision support. We introduce DentaCoPilot, a recommender that returns a calibrated top-K distribution over Current Dental Terminology (CDT) codes, a verbalised confidence label, an explicit abstain flag, and a chart-grounded rationale, and we compare four classical baselines with six LLM variants. On an apples-to-apples synthetic evaluation, classical baselines reach 0.567 top-1 while pure LLM variants trail at 0.267 to 0.467, and moving the backbone from Sonnet to Opus does not help. Prompt-conditioning a Sonnet LLM on the classical baselines candidate list closes most of the gap while preserving rationale and abstention; because the synthetic generator shares Markov structure with the bigram baseline, these synthetic rankings index pipeline behaviour, not externally valid clinical performance. We anchor external validity for the task on the public MEPS 2023 corpus (11,016 next-visit transitions across 5,088 patients): a history-aware model reaches 0.479 top-1 against 0.297 for a most-frequent baseline, is well calibrated (expected calibration error 0.031), and supports split-conformal selective prediction with a distribution-free coverage guarantee. A zero-shot LLM given only 1 this coarse history loses to the same classical baseline, so the LLMs value depends on rich charts rather than category history alone. Prospective clinician-in-the-loop validation and CDT-level real-data validation remain future work, contingent on clinical-data access and ethics approval that we have sought but not secured.

Deep Learning Trained Without Target-Domain Labels Improves Risk Stratification Over Expert Coronary Calcium Reading on Routine Attenuation Correction CT

Shanbhag, A.; Miller, R. J.; Killekar, A.; Marcinkiewicz, A. M.; Zhou, J.; Lemley, M.; Kamagate, A.; Van Kriekinge, S. D.; Kavanagh, P. B.; Feher, A.; Miller, E. J.; Liang, J. X.; Berman, D. S.; Dey, D.; Leahy, R. M.; Slomka, P.

Coronary artery calcium (CAC) is an established measure of coronary atherosclerosis on computed tomography (CT). Many SPECT and most PET myocardial perfusion studies already include a low-dose CT scan from which coronary calcium could be reported, yet it almost never is. CAC may not be reported due to image noise, cardiac motion, or the absence of a reliable reference standard. Deep learning (DL) models trained on these low-dose CT scans inherit the same uncertainty, because their reference labels are drawn on the degraded images. We bypassed this limitation by training a DL CAC model entirely outside the target domain: expert labels from high-quality gated CT were inherited by synthetic ungated images derived from those same scans, so that no CTAC scan was annotated, and no real CTAC image was used, at any point in training. Models were trained using one center and externally tested in two other centers. Because each synthetic image is derived from the gated scan itself, expert gated segmentations remain valid voxel-for-voxel without registration. Using these pairs, we developed a cross-domain knowledge adaptation model (knowledge adaptation, KAD-CAC). The DL models were externally tested on 5969 patients with a median age of 64 (IQR 56 - 73), of whom 50.2% were male. The KAD-CAC model had higher Cohens kappa K (0.86, 95% CI 0.84 - 0.88) compared to a convolutional LSTM model (K 0.78, 95% CI 0.76 - 0.80, p<0.01), or models trained with only gated images (K 0.81, 95% CI 0.80 - 0.82, p<0.01). Net reclassification improvement for death or myocardial infarction over a clinical and perfusion baseline was greatest for the KAD-CAC model and exceeded that of expert CAC reading on the same CTAC scans (difference 0.089, 95% CI 0.051 to 0.125, p<0.001). A model trained without any labels from CTAC scans therefore stratified risk of death or MI better than expert reading of the same scans. Patients classified as CAC 0 by KAD-CAC had lower adjusted risk of death or MI than patients classified as CAC 0 by experts (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.25 - 0.98). We use paired synthetic ungated scans to transfer expert gated CAC annotations into the ungated domain, resulting in better vessel-level CAC scoring and improved risk reclassification compared with expert reading of the same scans. Automated CAC scoring could be added to every myocardial perfusion imaging report without additional imaging or radiation. The approach provides a general route to expert-quality supervision for any acquisition in which the native reference standard is unreliable.

Proteotyping thresholds for APOE {varepsilon}4 carrier status and copy number were derived in ADRC and externally validated in the other cohorts. Logistic, proportional-odds, and Cox proportional-hazards models compared genotype- and proteotype-defined groups in relation to amyloid positivity, cross-sectional CDR global scores, and time to CDR-SB worsening, respectively. ResultsPlasma APOE4 accurately classified APOE {varepsilon}4 carrier status in ADRC (AUC=0.977, 95%CI 0.967-0.985; threshold 5.78 NULISA protein quantification [NPQ] value) and achieved near-perfect external validation in HeartSCORE, LATTICE, and ALPS (all AUCs=1.000, 95%CI 1.000-1.000). Plasma APOE4 also well-differentiated heterozygous from homozygous {varepsilon}4 carriers (ADRC AUC=0.879, 95%CI 0.834-0.921; threshold 12.23 NPQ value). Genotype- and proteotype-defined {varepsilon}4 carrier status showed similar associations with amyloid positivity (OR=4.40 vs 4.45), higher CDR global scores (OR=1.50 for both), and CDR-SB worsening (HR=1.22 for both). DiscussionPlasma APOE4 proteotyping accurately distinguished genotype-defined APOE {varepsilon}4 carrier status and copy number while demonstrating AD-related associations comparable to DNA genotyping.
