Automating postmarketing pharmacovigilance with large language model agents

Zhang, R.; Zhou, Y.; Chui, C. S. L.; Yu, D.; Wu, Z.; Luo, R.

Postmarketing pharmacovigilance relies on spontaneous adverse event reporting and labor-intensive observational studies, which suffer from underreporting and delayed risk detection. Large language model (LLM) agents could automate real-world evidence generation, but generalist agents make ad hoc analytical decisions that compromise epidemiological validity and reproducibility. Here we present MedCheckpoint, a multi-agent LLM system for automated postmarketing pharmacovigilance. MedCheckpoint grounds each analytical stage in a curated, source-traceable medical knowledge base, locks core study-design parameters before execution, performs patient-level analyses with human-written deterministic statistical tools, and enforces checkpoint rules that abort infeasible analyses, with optional human-in-the-loop review. We evaluated MedCheckpoint using a 54-question benchmark on a dual-purpose simulated electronic health record (EHR) testbed of 2.7 million individuals, designed with reliable clinical signals to evaluate routine analytical accuracy alongside spurious signals to stress-test system rigor. Across the benchmark, MedCheckpoint outperformed two coding-agent baselines in report quality across five dimensions rated by three independent LLM judges (overall means 4.81-4.88 versus 4.25-4.55 and 4.02-4.29) and yielded higher run-to-run consistency (mean within-question standard deviation 0.116-0.186 versus up to 0.440). Crucially, while baselines failed on up to 2 of 21 runs when handling infeasible comparisons or unmeasurable outcomes, MedCheckpoint produced no misleading failures across all seven stress-test trap questions with known data limitations. MedCheckpoint enables reliable, auditable, and scalable pharmacovigilance analysis.

Benchmark Self-Triage (BeST): Comparing Self-Triage Systems and Multiple Generations of OpenAI Models Against Preliminary Expert Consensus

Kroll, L. E.; Seidel, A.; Schuster, J.; Steiger, E.; Schoepke, T.; von Stillfried, D.; BeST Study Group,

As digital self-triage becomes more widely used, quality assurance requires evaluation of both emergency recognition and overall agreement with clinical judgement, including how these outcomes differ across generations of large language models. The Benchmark Self-Triage (BeST) project intends to provide a framework to compare systems for digital self-triage against a medical gold standard in Germany. As a first step in developing and demonstrating the methodology, we compared study-specific implementations of SmED 5.4.11 accessed via API and Infermedica Engine API via Microsoft Azure Healthcare Agent Service with five structured OpenAI configurations spanning multiple model generations: gpt-4.1, gpt-5.4, gpt-5.6-luna, gpt-5.6-sol, and gpt-6-astra. The manufacturers of SmED and Infermedica offer MDR-certified Class IIb triage products, but these certified products were not directly evaluated. An LLM-based patient actor covered 480 physician-curated vignettes derived from German routine triage data. A pool of seven physicians provided preliminary reference classifications and consensus for 480 vignettes. Recommendations were mapped to three urgency categories-emergency, acute, and not urgent-and evaluated using winsorised weights derived from routine triage frequencies. Weighted emergency sensitivity ranged from 51.3% (95% CI 35.566.3%) for gpt-4.1 to 99.9% (99.7-100.0%) for gpt-6-astra. Weighted exact three-category agreement (accuracy) ranged from 54.4% to 68.8%; gpt-4.1 achieved the highest agreement despite the lowest emergency sensitivity. No system performed best across all urgency categories. The later-generation OpenAI configurations showed a more precautionary triage profile resembling SmED, with higher sensitivity for emergency cases; gpt-6-astra showed the strongest shift. This cross-sectional comparison across model generations suggests a shift towards more precautionary triage behaviour, without establishing a longitudinal improvement in accuracy. These findings highlight the limitations of overall agreement for evaluating self-triage and support category-specific assessment. Broader expert consensus and evaluation, as well as further steps to reduce dependence on SmED-derived source data, are needed and planned before drawing conclusions about clinical safety.

Generative AI Versus Primary Care Physicians from Plan Initiation to Long-Term Maintenance of Chronic Disease Self-Management: A Vignette-Based Validation Study

Zhao, Z.; Yang, H.; Wang, Q.; Wang, Y.; Cheng, W.; Ma, X.

BackgroundGenerative artificial intelligence (GenAI) may provide personalised and dynamically adjustable support for chronic disease self-management. However, previous studies have largely focused on isolated questions and a limited range of evaluation dimensions. ObjectiveTo compare the quality of self-management advice generated by GenAI and primary care physicians (PCPs) across multiple stages of chronic disease management and evaluation dimensions. MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional, source-blinded comparative evaluation using 15 standardised vignettes of diabetes and hypertension derived from authentic primary care follow-up records. Each vignette comprised three sequential management stages, with medical accuracy, readability, and behaviour-change potential assessed across the three simulated stages; individualisation and multidisciplinary integration at plan initiation; contextual adaptability during acute adjustment; and empathy during long-term maintenance. The GenAI system and 22 PCPs independently generated advice which was independently rated by three clinicians, one each from general practice, endocrinology, and cardiology. across seven dimensions. Linear mixed-effects models were used for the primary comparisons, and case-level paired and physician-distribution analyses were conducted as robustness analyses. ResultsThe GenAI system achieved estimated marginal means above 4.0 in all dimensions except readability. It scored significantly higher than PCPs in six of the seven dimensions, with adjusted mean differences ranging from 1.16 to 1.56 points (P<.001). Readability was the only exception, with no significant difference between GenAI and PCPs (-0.22, 95% CI -0.54 to 0.11; P=.19). The GenAI mean exceeded the individual means of all recruited PCPs in the six dimensions but ranked at approximately the 23rd percentile for readability. ConclusionsOur findings indicated the GenAI system may generate more highly rated self-management advice than PCPs across most evaluated dimensions but offered no advantage in readability. These findings provide pre-implementation evidence for GenAI as a potential source of structured and adaptive chronic disease self-management support. Patient-level pilot studies and subsequent randomised trials are needed to determine its acceptability and effects on behaviour and clinical outcomes.

AToP: An Explainability Framework for Transformer-Based EHR Predictions Using Attribution-Guided Temporal Pattern Mining

Belouali, A.; Kharrazi, H.

ObjectiveTo develop and evaluate AToP (Attribution to Temporal Patterns), a post-hoc explainability framework that converts per-token Integrated Gradients (IG) from a Transformer into structured temporal pattern narratives. Materials and MethodsAToP was applied to a single-stream Transformer trained on MIMIC-IV v3.1 (218,196 patients; 12.6% unplanned 30-day readmission). It mines frequent episodes from salient-token subsequences in the training cohort, aggregates attribution among patients whose records contain each token or pattern, and collapses near-duplicates by patient overlap. Validation used masking, reversed-order analysis, token-level faithfulness tests, and KernelSHAP comparison. ResultsThe Transformer achieved AUROC 0.77 and PR-AUC 0.34. Population-weighted attribution emphasized high-prevalence codes with small per-patient impact. Carrier-conditional attribution, the mean among patients with the token, surfaced rare high-attribution psychiatric markers including psychosis, self-harm overdose, suicidal ideation (SI), and severe major depressive disorder (MDD). Pattern-level attribution identified within-visit co-occurrences and cross-visit trajectories not captured by token rankings. Risk-increasing patterns included psychiatric sequences ({MDD, SI} [-&gt;] SI) and oncologic progressions (cancer anemia [-&gt;] ascites). Obstetric and chest pain evaluation patterns were associated with lower predicted readmission. DiscussionThe framework provides two complementary views of model predictions: carrier-conditional aggregation highlights rare, highly attributed clinical features, while temporal pattern analysis identifies within-visit combinations and cross-visit trajectories missed by token-level rankings. Masking and visit-order reversal provided evidence of correspondence with model behavior, but pattern attributions do not represent unique effects or establish clinical validity. ConclusionAToP turns a Transformers attributions into visit-anchored temporal pattern narratives, showing which events, in which order, contribute to its readmission predictions.

Silent Drift and Site-level Variance in Clinical AI: Real-world performance and subgroup analysis for post-market monitoring of an FDA-cleared artificial intelligence (AI) triage tool for high-risk use case of pulmonary embolism

Dayma, K.; Newton, C.; Kawzowicz, M.; Patel, P.; Curl, R.; Ogden, K.

BackgroundFDA-cleared artificial intelligence (AI) triage tool for pulmonary embolism (PE) detection has been widely used; however, its performance has not been analyzed for subgroups, clinical sites, and temporal stability. MethodsThis retrospective, six-site health system study evaluated the Aidoc BriefCase PE triage model (K190072) across 14,736 CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) examinations between July 2023 and March 2026. Reference-standard PE characteristic labels were extracted from radiology reports using a zero-shot LLM pipeline validated against a radiologist-annotated 600 reports ({kappa} =0.98). Model performance was evaluated across demographics, embolus characteristics, and site. Temporal behavior was evaluated on operating point by logistic regression, with and without site adjustment, and by segmented regression. ResultsPE was present in 1,402/14,736 examinations (9.5%). Overall sensitivity was 64.1% (95% CI, 61.5-66.6%), specificity 98.8% (95% CI, 98.6-99.0%), compared with 93% sensitivity and 93.7% specificity in the FDA 510(k) summary. Sensitivity fell with peripherality (central 88.6%, lobar or segmental 63.1%, subsegmental 33.3%) and with chronicity (acute 65.5%, chronic 57.4%). Sensitivity differed significantly between the two highest-volume sites, 68.7% and 55.7% (P < .001), with CT scanner as the only variable different between the two sites. The false-positive rate fell 40% per year (P<.001) at a similar rate at two sites, while sensitivity fell 15% per year at site A (P<.036), from 72.5% to 65.0% between two halves of the study period. The manufacturer did not report model updates or threshold changes during the study period. ConclusionsDeployed sensitivity was substantially below the cleared benchmark and varied 13 points by site. The gradual shift in high specificity at the cost of low sensitivity, either from data drift or threshold change, either not monitored or not documented by the manufacturer, highlights the importance of independent continuous post-market monitoring.

Primary Care Quality and Inappropriate Community Antibiotic Use: Evidence from Linked Provider and Household Data

Chen, Y.; Yi, H.; Rao, S.; Weber, A.; Hassmiller-Lich, K.; Sylvia, S.

Inappropriate antibiotic use presents a major global health challenge, particularly in resource-limited settings where access to quality care remains limited while access to antibiotics is relatively unrestricted. This study estimates the relationship between frontline primary care quality and community antibiotic use, drawing on detailed data from approximately 100 rural villages in China that link objective measures of village doctors clinical practice quality, assessed through unannounced standardized patient visits, to household-level antibiotic use data measured within the same communities. To mitigate potential endogeneity concerns, we employed an instrumental variable (IV) approach integrated within a double/debiased machine learning (DML) framework, using ensemble machine learning to combine information across multiple individually weak but jointly informative provider-level instruments. Higher clinical practice quality was associated with lower probabilities of inappropriate antibiotic receipt during healthcare encounters for cold or diarrhea symptoms (OLS: 6.2 percentage points; DML-IV: 9.6 percentage points) and households storing antibiotics at home for future use (OLS: 4.1 percentage points; DML-IV: 10.9 percentage points). These findings highlight the potential role of improving frontline primary care quality in community antibiotic stewardship. Methodologically, this study illustrates how causal machine learning can complement conventional econometric methods by addressing high-dimensional estimation challenges in global health economics research.

ImportancePayment policy for clinical artificial intelligence (AI) is changing quickly, yet national data on which AI-enabled services are used, and for whom, are limited. ObjectiveTo describe national trends in the use of AI-related Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes and how use differs by patient, geographic, and payer characteristics. Main Outcomes and MeasuresEncounters with any of 65 CPT codes that require AI, identified by matching the CPT code set to US Food and Drug Administration-listed AI-enabled devices and classified as assistive, augmentative, or autonomous using the CPT Appendix S taxonomy. ResultsOf 11.6 billion encounters, 335,349 (2.9 per 100,000) included an AI-required CPT code. As the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services restructures payment for software and launches outcome-based models, tracking who receives AI-enabled care across payment pathways will be important for equitable access. KEY POINTSO_ST_ABSQuestionC_ST_ABSHow has billed use of artificial intelligence (AI)-related CPT codes changed nationally since 2021, and how does use differ by patient, geographic, and payer characteristics? FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 11.6 billion US encounters, 2.9 per 100,000 included an AI-required CPT code. Odds of use were nearly 10 times higher in 2025 than in 2021, higher among Medicare beneficiaries, and lower among Medicaid enrollees and residents of socially vulnerable areas. MeaningBilled AI use remains rare and uneven across payers and communities, so payment policy for AI may need to be paired with monitoring of who receives AI-enabled care.
