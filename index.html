Interpretable Deep Learning Models for Breast Cancer Diagnosis Using Medical Imaging

Sami Alnawali, Muhammad Atif Saeed, Abdul Hadi, Azka Atiq, Akhtar Jamil, Ahmad Din

Deep learning shows promise for breast cancer diagnosis, but limited interpretability hinders clinical adoption. We benchmark ten ImageNet-pretrained models (ResNet50, VGG19, InceptionV3, DenseNet121, Xception, MobileNetV2, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt, ViT, and Swin Transformer) for benign-versus-malignant classification on the BreakHis histopathology dataset using a patient-level split, and explain their predictions with Grad-CAM, LIME, Integrated Gradients, and LRP. DenseNet121 performed best, with 99.4% test accuracy and an AUC-ROC of 0.999, and significantly outperformed all other models (p < 0.001). Transformer-based models trailed the CNNs. The explanations consistently highlighted nuclei-rich and glandular regions of diagnostic relevance. Combining transfer learning with explainable AI can thus yield accurate, interpretable tools for breast cancer diagnosis.

ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents

Sirry Chen, Fangke Chen, Xuanwen Ding, Wei Chen, Zhongyu Wei

Disease diagnosis is a key application of large language model (LLM) agents in healthcare. However, existing medical agents are primarily trained and evaluated with static question-answering data, providing limited opportunities to learn active information acquisition, examination decision-making, and dynamic clinical interaction. To address this limitation, we present ClinVerse, an interactive, real-data-grounded multi-agent environment designed for training and evaluating medical diagnostic agents. Built on ClinPatient, a scalable dataset comprising 150k diagnostic cases derived from real-world electronic health records, ClinVerse supports coordinated interactions among six specialized clinical roles. Specifically, a doctor agent starts from an initial patient complaint, progressively gathers clinical evidence through patient interviews and examination requests, and ultimately produces a diagnosis. For each interaction trajectory, a rule-based scoring framework quantifies information acquisition, examination ordering, and diagnostic accuracy. These trajectory-level scores serve as reward signals for agent training while also enabling scalable evaluation. In experiments, we evaluate various general-purpose and medical-domain models and show that nine self-evolution methods across three categories consistently improve agents' diagnostic capabilities within ClinVerse. We further analyze the interplay among model capability, environment feedback, and self-evolution methods.

Radiology Guideline-derived Decision Graph for Efficient Clinical Decision Support

Xuewen Zhou, Zhen Chen

Radiological risk stratification and management planning are essential downstream tasks in medical imaging. Automating these tasks is challenging because clinical guideline criteria are distributed across prose, tables, and cross-referenced definitions. We present Guideline-derived Decision Graph (GDGraph), a framework that decouples guideline compilation from case evaluation. During compilation, a large language model partitions guidelines into section-based chunks, extracts local decision subgraphs with linked definitions, and aggregates related branches into an executable decision graph. During inference, a fast System One model answers localized clinical questions while deterministic traversal resolves Boolean logic, turning opaque single-pass predictions into a traceable decision path without task-specific training. Across 347 cases spanning renal cyst, adnexal mass, and thyroid nodule guidelines, GDGraph improves System One accuracy from 57.3% to 90.8%, outperforming retrieval-augmented GPT-4o (80.1%) and graph-guided GPT-4o with chain-of-thought (82.7%) at substantially lower latency and cost. The source code will be available.

A 211K Parameter Foundation Model: Genetic Prompting in Neural Cellular Automata

John Kalkhof, Niklas Ihm, Daniel Kirste, Anirban Mukhopadhyay

The scale of modern foundation models enables broad task generalization. In medical imaging, where segmentation demands vary widely across clinical contexts, such flexibility is invaluable. But current models, such as SAM trade adaptability for scale, locking their utility behind high-end infrastructure and leaving behind the low-resource environments most in need of diagnostic support. To bridge this gap, we introduce Foundational-NCA, a lightweight foundation model capable of adapting to a wide range of medical segmentation tasks without retraining. Built on Neural Cellular Automata (NCA), Foundational-NCA incorporates gradient-based learning with genetic optimization over a latent task space, using a compact task vector to guide predictions. This enables dynamic adaptation via a genetic prompt mechanism, allowing the model to generalize across tasks while keeping the parameter count magnitudes lower at just 211 thousand, compared to SAM-based models with 38.9 to 636 million parameters. We evaluate Foundational-NCA on 15 trained and 6 entirely unseen segmentation tasks from the TotalSegmentator dataset, achieving an average Dice score of 84.5% on known tasks and 60.4% on unseen ones. These results demonstrate that task generalization is possible in ultra-compact models, laying the groundwork for resource-efficient, deployable foundation models in clinical environments where infrastructure is limited. By reducing model size by over 180 times, Foundational-NCA takes a step toward a more equitable future for medical AI, one where powerful tools are within reach of every healthcare setting.

Chenchen Wang, You Zhou

Medical image segmentation is crucial for clinical diagnosis and treatment planning, requiring both global contextual understanding and fine-grained structural precision. While the Segment Anything Model (SAM) demonstrates strong generalization capabilities, its direct application to medical images often lacks domain-specific adaptability. Meanwhile, State Space Models (SSMs)-based methods, such as Mamba, efficiently capture long-range dependencies but may struggle with preserving fine anatomical details. To address these challenges, we propose a segment anything model based context guided mamba unet, SAMBA-UNet, a novel framework that integrates SAM's context knowledge with Mamba's hierarchical feature learning to enhance both global representation and local detail refinement. Specifically, we introduce the Hierarchical Context Enhancement Module (HCEM), an attention-based fusion module that deeply incorporates the multi-level features of SAM and Mamba to improve detail perception and segmentation precision. Additionally, we employ LoRA and Adapter-based fine-tuning to adapt SAM to medical imaging tasks, further enhancing its domain-specific segmentation capability and robustness. We conducted a comprehensive study on several medical segmentation datasets, including Synapse, ISIC17, and ISIC18, evaluating our method across multiple metrics. Experimental results demonstrate that SAMBA-UNet achieves state-of-the-art performance, effectively balancing generalization, segmentation accuracy, and computational efficiency. Our code will be released upon acceptance.

Large Language Models for Accurate Medical Chart Abstraction: Enabling Scalable and Secure Artificial Intelligence Deployment in Stroke

Zhusi Zhong, Carl M. Porto, David Hong, Miranda Gonzales, Revyn Kim, Gnaneswari Karayi

Medical chart abstraction transforms unstructured clinical narratives into structured variables for research and quality monitoring. We evaluated a prompting-based large language model (LLM) framework for extracting eight clinical variables from 2,416 neurointerventional acute stroke intervention reports across three hospitals. Twenty-two instruction-tuned open-source LLMs were evaluated across architectures, model sizes, and biomedical adaptations using Quick Response and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. Non-expert staff annotations served as the cross-model reference standard. Evaluation was restricted to report-variable pairs with a valid LLaMA3.3-70B CoT prediction and a corresponding reference label. Performance measures included extraction accuracy, inference latency, and agreement with expert adjudication. Twenty discordant cases per variable underwent review by two senior physicians and four medical students. LLaMA3.3-70B achieved the highest overall accuracy (94.8%). CoT improved performance on inference-dependent variables such as site of occlusion, while Quick Response was optimal for directly stated procedural fields. Expert adjudication showed that AI predictions were more closely aligned with expert interpretations than non-expert annotations for most variables, with intracranial angioplasty remaining a challenge. Locally deployed LLMs can support scalable clinical information extraction and retrospective stroke registry curation, with expert oversight for ambiguous cases.

Mara Pleasure, Ekaterina Redekop, Vedrana Ivezic, Rushikesh Kulkarni, Aaron Chin, William Speier

Foundation models trained on longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs) can predict clinical diagnoses years in the future, making them a potential tool for disease screening. We developed TRACER (Temporal Risk Assessment with Clinical EHR Representations), a decoder-based foundation model pretrained on data from over 4.5 million patients from four health systems, and evaluated it on 16 incident diseases at 1-, 2-, and 5-year horizons in more than 2 million patients from two health systems excluded from model development. Evaluating at this operating point also revealed what global metrics alone did not: detection and false-alert burden varied across diseases and health systems, even when discrimination appeared stable. Our results illustrate the potential of foundation models for screening and underscore the need for evaluation in health systems excluded from development to assess disease detection and false-alert burden at levels appropriate for intended clinical use.}

MedJev: Evaluating Zero-Shot System-One Decision Making in Medicine

Simon A. Lee, Jiahang Sha, Moonseong Jeong, Yunbei Pan, Jeffrey N. Chiang

System One decision models such as Jev answer structured questions in a single forward pass without generating text, offering fast and computationally efficient clinical decision support. We evaluate MedJev, an open System One model that runs locally on a laptop, across ten medical benchmarks spanning medical knowledge, diagnosis, and patient level prediction using MIMIC IV and MIMIC IV ED. We compare MedJev against zero shot LLM baselines, published GPT results, and supervised models. MedJev achieves accuracies of 72.5 percent on MedQA, 75.4 percent on USMLE, and 80.1 percent on NEJM, approaching published GPT 4 performance while maintaining well calibrated confidence estimates (ECE 0.028 to 0.063). On patient level tasks, MedJev achieves an AUROC of 0.926 for In Hospital Mortality, exceeding XGBoost (0.902), but underperforms supervised models on operational outcomes such as ED Triage Admission (0.745 vs. 0.809). Patient level predictions also exhibit substantial miscalibration (In Hospital Mortality ECE 0.286). We introduce a lightweight, two parameter recalibration method that adapts predictions to local patient populations using 50 to 200 labeled examples, reducing ECE to 0.007. Our findings establish open System One models as a promising zero shot baseline for medical AI, combining competitive predictive performance with low computational cost and efficient local calibration. We release our code and evaluation harness to support future research.

Ontology-guided multi-agent reasoning for explainable differential diagnosis

Roxana Ramona Szomiu, George Ursu, Adrian Groza

Effective differential diagnosis depends on well structured and unambiguous patient case descriptions, since incomplete or ambiguous clinical information readily leads to diagnostic errors and inconsistent reasoning. Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently shown promising capabilities in medical question answering and clinical reasoning; however, even when achieving competitive performance on medical benchmarks, they often rely on pattern recognition learned from training data rather than grounded clinical evidence, and can generate plausible yet unsupported diagnostic conclusions. Their reasoning remains largely opaque, making it difficult for clinicians to assess the evidence and rationale behind a given recommendation. To address this gap, we propose an ontology-guided, multiagent differential diagnosis framework that replicates the iterative reasoning process of physicians. The framework grounds diagnostic inference in a structured, expert-curated medical knowledge base, enabling interpretation of symptoms, clinical findings, or disease relationships, while decomposing diagnosis into explicit, auditable reasoning steps. This ontology-based grounding constrains inference to evidence-backed associations, reducing hallucination and improving diagnostic transparency, traceability, and consistency. The system then identifies the most discriminative missing symptoms and generates targeted follow-up questions to reduce uncertainty through a process that mirrors to clinical inquiry. Together, these components improve the reliability, explainability, and diagnostic accuracy of AI-assisted differential diagnosis. The source code is publicly available.

Psychotherapy science, however, remains constrained by methodological limitations, resource-intensive trials, challenges in manipulating clinically meaningful features experimentally, and a potential ceiling in efficacy. Leveraging generative AI and large language models (LLMs), we propose a framework for "in silico psychotherapy research": controlled simulations of psychotherapeutic interactions under reproducible experimental conditions. ObjectiveWe test whether such simulation experiments can recover a theoretically expected effect of an isolated psychotherapy-process manipulation. MethodsIn a preregistered proof-of-concept study, we generated 512 fully automated motivational interviewing (MI) sessions by linking locally deployed Llama-3.3-70B-In-struct based therapist and patient models across eight vignettes and eight therapist personas. Therapist, but not patient, prompts manipulated common therapeutic factors (CTF), contrasting high versus low empathy, affirmation, and alliance building behaviour in a controlled design. The primary outcome was MI treatment integrity (MITI 4.2 global ratings), assessed by a blinded LLM with repeated ratings and analysed using linear mixed-effects models. Secondary outcomes included observer-inferred importance/confidence rulers based on simulated patient utterances; Therapist Empathy Scale (TES) and Working Alliance Inventory-Short Observer (WAI-S-O) served as manipulation fidelity checks. Human coders rated a stratified subsample. ResultsOf the 512 simulations, 506 (98.8%) yielded complete transcripts and 480 (93.75) were retained for analysis. ConclusionsLLM-based psychotherapy simulations were technically feasible, demonstrated experimental fidelity, and showed theoretically concordant effects beyond directly manipulated constructs and simulation participants. These findings provide proof of concept for in silico psychotherapy research as a promising complementary pre-clinical experimental paradigm for hypothesis generation and testing to support accelerated intervention development. The applicability of this approach across patient populations, therapeutic modalities and model architectures requires further evaluation.
