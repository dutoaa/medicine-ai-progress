Prediction of incident inflammatory bowel disease from routine electronic health records: development and external validation of a longitudinal machine learning model

Onal, G.; Ayati, A.; Chen, M.-Y.; Mukherjee, A.; Rudrapatna, V. A.

BACKGROUND & AIMSInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is often diagnosed months to years after symptoms begin, and diagnostic delay is associated with complications. We tested whether longitudinal machine learning using electronic health record (EHR) data can identify incident IBD before diagnosis, and quantified lead time and deployment burden. METHODSWe studied adults with an IBD-compatible symptom, sign, or finding at UCSF (2012-2024). Incident cases were confirmed by structured filtering and validated large language model note review. We trained a state space model (Mamba) and comparators on pre-diagnostic EHR data, excluding diagnostic-workup features, and assessed discrimination at 6- and 12-month landmarks. We externally tested Mamba without recalibration at ZSFG, a safety-net hospital serving a demographically and socioeconomically distinct population, and simulated diagnosis-code-only deployment at UCSF. RESULTSSix months after presentation, among patients still undiagnosed (67% of UCSF test cases), Mamba achieved an AUROC of 0.969 (95% CI, 0.928-0.999) at UCSF and 0.965 (0.952-0.976) at ZSFG. Median lead time was 20 months at UCSF and 15 months at ZSFG. Among future cases with an initially normal colonoscopy (N=32) or a histological finding of acute inflammation without chronicity (N=63), the model flagged 84% and 89% of these patients, respectively, before those procedures, indicating the potential for pre-diagnostic detection. Among 358,886 symptomatic patients not used for training (60 incident cases), the diagnosis-code-only model achieved an AUROC of 0.926; reviewing 20 alerts/month identified 34 cases (PPV, 1.09%; 64-fold above baseline). CONCLUSIONSLongitudinal machine learning can identify increased IBD risk months to years before diagnosis and may define a population for prospective studies of early IBD biology. Prospective evaluation should determine whether earlier identification reduces diagnostic delay. What You Need to KnowO_ST_ABSBackground and ContextC_ST_ABSIBD is often diagnosed months to years after symptoms begin. Whether routinely collected electronic health record data can identify future IBD before diagnosis, and at what alert burden, is unknown. New FindingsA longitudinal model identified future IBD six months after first symptomatic presentation with an AUROC of 0.97 and a median lead time of 20 months, with similar external discrimination. LimitationsThis was a retrospective study; external case labels were inferred rather than adjudicated, and colonoscopy analyses were exploratory. Whether earlier flagging shortens time to diagnosis requires prospective evaluation. Clinical Research RelevanceRoutinely collected structured EHR data could support targeted gastroenterology review of recurrently symptomatic patients. Because incident IBD is rare among symptomatic patients, a service reviewing 20 flagged charts per month would identify roughly one future case every five months. Deployment should therefore begin in silent mode, with locally calibrated thresholds and prospective measurement of alert burden. Basic Research RelevanceThe model flagged most future cases before a pre-diagnostic colonoscopy read as normal, or before biopsies showing only acute inflammation. Patients flagged despite normal-appearing mucosa may represent a preclinical population in which the earliest immunologic, serologic, and microbial events of IBD could be studied prospectively, including through model-guided mucosal sampling. Lay SummaryAn AI system reviewing medical records data is able to identify people who would later be diagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease, often more than a year before doctors made the diagnosis.

Can GPT Be Used as an Alternative Prediction Model to Traditional Machine Learning and Neural Networks on Low-Volume Clinical Data?

Bin Akter, S.; Akter, S.; Eisenberg, D.; Hill, C.; Lotvola, A.; Fresneda Fernandez, J.; Sarkar Pias, T.; Rafiqul Islam, M.; Islam, H.

Background and ObjectiveEarly and reliable disease prediction from structured clinical data remains challenging when datasets are small, highly imbalanced, and contain limited positive disease cases. Conventional machine learning (ML) and deep learning approaches often struggle to capture clinically meaningful relationships under such low-data representation conditions due to weak statistical associations between features and prediction targets. This study proposes a clinically grounded Distil GPT2-based table-to-text framework for disease prediction using structured healthcare datasets, motivated by the contextual reasoning capability of GPT models to better capture clinically meaningful relationships when statistical learning alone becomes insufficient due to limited data availability. Methods & MaterialsStructured clinical records were transformed into physician-style textual descriptions and enriched through GPT4-generated medical paraphrasing to improve minority-class representation while preserving clinical meaning. Both the original and generated clinical texts were used to fine-tune a Distil GPT2 model across four public healthcare datasets, including heart disease, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and thyroid cancer recurrence. Gradient-based explainable AI analysis was additionally incorporated to identify clinically important features influencing prediction outcomes. ResultsThe proposed framework demonstrated consistently strong predictive performance across four clinical datasets, achieving average precision, specificity, sensitivity, and F1-score of 0.96, 0.97, 0.96, and 0.96, respectively. The model achieved improved sensitivity, stronger generalization, and more stable predictive behavior compared with traditional ML, deep learning, transformer-based, and GAN-augmented approaches. Importantly, the framework consistently emphasized clinically meaningful variables even under severe class imbalance, where conventional ML and neural network models often struggled to identify key clinically relevant relationships. ConclusionsThe proposed Distil GPT2-based table-to-text framework provides a practical and clinically interpretable approach for disease prediction from limited structured healthcare data. By integrating contextual clinical reasoning with explainable prediction mechanisms, the framework suggests strong potential for early risk detection, transparent clinical decision support, and reliable deployment in real-world data-scarce healthcare settings.

MRI Biomarkers for Brain Metastases in Lung Cancer Using Machine Learning

Chadha, S.; Sritharan, D. V.; Sharma, R.; Dolezal, D.; Chande, S.; Breuer, G.; Fu, D.; Gupta, A.; Hossain, J. O.; Srinivasan, A.; Weiss, D.; Chiang, V. L.; Nguyen, D. X.; Aneja, S.

PurposeExisting prognostic models for brain metastases from lung cancer rely on clinical variables and do not capture the biological heterogeneity of host or tumor factors underlying survival outcomes. We evaluated whether MRI-derived radiomic biomarkers improve prognostic stratification beyond clinical variables. We also investigated their biological correlates using matched histopathology. Experimental DesignThis retrospective study included 103 patients with brain metastases from lung cancer and matched pre-operative MRI and histopathology. Radiomic features from T1-weighted contrast-enhanced and T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequences were combined with clinical variables to train Cox proportional hazards, random survival forest, and DeepSurv models for overall survival (OS), CNS progression, and leptomeningeal (LM) progression. Risk groups from an imaging-only model were compared for clinical and histopathologic differences. ResultsImaging features improved prognostic performance beyond clinical variables across all modeling frameworks, with the combined clinical-imaging DeepSurv model achieving the highest concordance index for OS (0.728). Risk groups from the best-performing model for each endpoint differed significantly in OS (median 26.7 vs. 9.1 months, p < 0.001) and LM progression (p = 0.003), but not in CNS progression. Imaging-only risk groups did not differ significantly in any clinical variable, but high-risk patients were less likely to have tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (46% vs. 77%, p = 0.010) and PD-L1 expression [&ge;] 50% (10% vs. 32%, p = 0.049). ConclusionsMRI radiomic biomarkers provide prognostic information beyond established clinical variables in lung cancer brain metastases and correspond to features of the tumor immune microenvironment, supporting their potential to refine risk stratification. Statement of Translational RelevanceBrain metastases affect 30-40% of patients with lung cancer. Existing prognostic models rely on clinical variables, which do not fully capture the biological heterogeneity that drives outcomes. We show that machine learning models integrating MRI-derived radiomic features with clinical variables improve prognostic discrimination beyond clinical variables alone. Because these biomarkers are derived from standard-of-care pre-operative imaging, they can be generated without additional testing or delay to treatment planning. Using matched histopathology from resected lesions, we further show that imaging-defined risk groups do not simply reproduce established clinical risk factors but instead correspond to differences in the tumor immune microenvironment, including tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and PD-L1 expression. These findings support the use of quantitative imaging biomarkers as a non-invasive method to refine risk stratification at diagnosis, informing treatment selection and clinical trial enrollment for patients with brain metastases from lung cancer.

Artificial Intelligence-Based Modeling to Aid in the Identification of Genetic Conditions in Infants with Congenital Heart Disease

Caiola, M.; Thompson, T.; Stephens, S. B.; Salamat, B.; Harris, K.; Lara, C.; Coban-Akdemir, Z.; Zhang, L.; Roth, M.; Milosavljevic, A.; Weitz, A. C.; Morris, S. A.

BackgroundGenetic conditions are common in congenital heart disease (CHD) and affect care, but are often missed or diagnosed late. We asked whether routine clinical data can shortlist the most likely genetic conditions. MethodsRetrospective single-center cohort of children with CHD and an echocardiogram who had either a confirmed genetic diagnosis or negative cytogenetic testing and CHD gene sequencing. Echocardiogram images and serial medical record data were added as model inputs in the order they become clinically available. Class-balanced logistic regression (LR) and a multimodal deep learning fusion network were fitted to the same inputs at each step. The primary metric was balanced top-3 accuracy (correct diagnosis among the top three, averaged across conditions), with bootstrap intervals. ResultsThe cohort included 1,720 children (988 with common CHD-associated genetic conditions, 231 rare diagnoses, 501 negative testing). Using only inputs generated at the echocardiogram visit, LR reached a balanced top-3 accuracy 0.758 (chance 0.429). Serial records, radiology notes and ICD-10 codes, raised this to 0.809. Multimodal fusion performed similarly at each stage. With a genetic condition withheld from training, the fusion embedding placed affected children nearest to others sharing a gene group in 0.37 of cases versus 0.10 without the embedding (+0.268 [95% CI +0.242, +0.295]). ConclusionsRoutine clinical data available at first echocardiogram can help identify likely genetic diagnoses, and later coded inputs ceiling. Multimodal fusion added no accuracy over LR, but places children sharing a genetic cause near one another, making nearest-neighbor retrieval informative for undiagnosed children.

FERMAT: An Autoregressive Clinical Foundation Model for Disease Risk and Clinical Event Prediction

Jung, J.; Lee, H.-C.

Clinical foundation models learn patterns in longitudinal health records that can be reused across prediction tasks. However, the relative strengths of representation-based and generation-based prediction across clinical tasks remain unclear. Using longitudinal electronic health records from 3.77 million patients at a single hospital, we developed FERMAT (Foundation model for Exploring Real-world Multimodal health data using Autoregressive Trajectory modeling), a 65-million-parameter clinical foundation model. We compared predictions from task-specific predictors trained on patient representations with those derived from generated clinical event sequences. Representation-based prediction performed better in the disease-risk comparisons. In detailed analyses of five-year chronic kidney disease, fatty liver, and diabetes risk in a test cohort of 10,000 patients, representation-based predictors exceeded generation-based estimates by 0.098-0.115 in observation-weighted AUROC and had lower probability error. Generation-based prediction, in turn, performed better in predicting diagnoses, prescriptions, and procedures recorded on the same day, a task motivated by the frequent presence of multiple events with the same date in hospital EHRs. Using 50 generated trajectories per patient, it achieved a Recall@10 of 71.0%, compared with 55.7% for representation-based prediction, with a paired improvement of 15.31 percentage points (95% CI, 13.92-16.79; n = 1,862). These findings demonstrate task-dependent strengths of representation-based and generation-based applications within a single clinical foundation model, providing an empirical basis for exploring how the two approaches can be used individually or combined to address clinical prediction needs.

RadKev: An Open-Weight Decision Model for Radiology

Ram, U.; Zhang, R.

BackgroundMany radiology decisions, such as whether a report describes a finding or how urgently a study should be read, are choices among a fixed set of answers. Decision models return a probability for each permitted answer in one step, without generating text, and an open-weight model can run within a hospital so that patient data stay local. No decision model has yet been specialized for radiology and compared with large language models (LLMs). MethodsWe fine-tuned the general-purpose decision models Kev-27B (Qwen3.8-27B backbone) and Kev-9B on 36,109 records from public radiology datasets to obtain RadKev-27B and RadKev-9B, and compared them with the Kev models, the hosted decision models Jev and OpenAI Decisions, and the LLM Qwen3.8-27B, on 14,142 held-out questions in fifteen tasks (report reading, case diagnosis and classification, radiological knowledge). The prespecified primary outcome was task-averaged accuracy relative to Kev-27B. ResultsRadKev-27B exceeded Kev-27B by 4.9 percentage points (95% CI 3.8 to 6.2), with the largest gain in report error detection (22.6 points); RadKev-9B exceeded Kev-9B by 6.9 points (5.5 to 8.4) and did not differ detectably from Kev-27B, a model three times its size. RadKev-27B was more accurate than the hosted decision models Jev (3.0 points, 1.7 to 4.4) and OpenAI Decisions (4.8, 3.2 to 6.4), than Qwen3.8-27B without reasoning (4.7, 3.4 to 6.0) and with reasoning (2.8 points, 1.0 to 4.6) on 1,675 questions, at 132-fold lower latency (128 ms vs 16.9 s per question). At an error rate of 5%, RadKev-27B could answer 90.5% of questions and Kev-27B 76.4%. ConclusionsRadKev-27B was more accurate than two hosted decision models and an LLM of the same size, with or without reasoning, at a fraction of the latency of reasoning, and can be deployed within an institution for categorical radiology decisions after validation on local data.

Introducing Infrastructure as Code for deployment and governance of medical AI: A case study

Vogel, M.; Reuter, N.; Ruf, S.; Noori, A.; Madrid, J.; Balicer, R.; Witzenrath, M.; Sander, L. E.; Groeschel, M. I.

Clinical AI deployment rarely reaches the bedside because both workstation integration and governed backend provisioning are challenging. We introduce a workflow that allows rapid and governed access to AI functionalities through a browser interface or API, enabling privacy-compliant data analysis. The complex setup for compliant healthcare applications such as a secure backend, private AI model endpoint, and managed identity configuration is encoded by itself, a concept known as Infrastructure as Code (IaC). IaC is a concept in which networks, access rights, and service endpoints are written down as machine-readable files that can be reviewed once and executed identically in any institution. Here, we apply this concept to a clinical use case. The process of deploying a governed, private clinical AI solution using this approach in a tertiary care center took, on average, 310.3 {+/-} 196.6 seconds (n=25, mean {+/-} SD). To further demonstrate the clinical value of this approach, we developed and deployed an LLM-based inconsistency checker, which identifies textual inconsistency in medical documents. When evaluating the tool on a set of 1,347 physician-approved clinical documents, the tool flagged a clinically severe inconsistency in 105 clinical documents (7.8%, approximately 1 in 13). In a severity-stratified physician audit, 149 of 150 sampled findings were confirmed (population-weighted PPV 98.2%; CI 94.8 - 100.0). In summary, we present the application of IaC frameworks to a healthcare use case and demonstrate that clinically useful AI applications can be deployed to physician workstations in hospital-governed cloud environments within a few minutes. DescriptionTo overcome the implementation barriers for use of AI in healthcare, we adapted Infrastructure as Code to a clinical use case and deployed a hospital-governed large language model service that clinicians reach from a browser without workstation software. Provisioned by Infrastructure as Code the service became available in 310 seconds on average, and its first clinical application, an inconsistency checker, detected inconsistencies in clinical documents with high positive predictive value.

Cross-database adaptation of machine learning models for predicting sustained antibiotic exposure among patients with intracerebral hemorrhage in intensive care units

Pathak, S.; Si, Y.; Sanjaya, J.; Haghi, M.; Kudrot, N. T.; Placencia, G.; Alaei, K.; Pishgar, M.

BackgroundPatients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) in intensive care units (ICUs) frequently receive antibiotics early in critical illness. Such exposure reflects suspected infection, physiological deterioration, monitoring intensity, and clinical management and may vary across institutions and electronic health record databases. We evaluated target-domain adaptation for modeling sustained antibiotic exposure in critically ill patients with ICH. MethodsWe analyzed derived ICH cohorts from MIMIC-IV and the eICU Collaborative Research Database. MIMIC-IV included 2,024 ICU stays and served as the source cohort. The eICU cohort comprised 1,277 stays and was divided, at the ICU-stay level, into a development subset (766 stays) and a held-out evaluation subset (511 stays). The eICU outcome was sustained antibiotic exposure on at least two ICU days. Candidate models incorporated demographic, comorbidity, physiological, laboratory, neurologic, respiratory, temporal, and measurementprocess features. Timestamped observations for static and temporal tabular families were censored at the recorded first-antibiotic time when available, whereas the compact sequence component and explicit measurement-density features used the full first 24 hours. For base-model families using eICU development data, meta-learner inputs were generated by five-fold stratified cross-fitting. A 42-input L2-penalized logistic meta-learner combined 16 model probabilities with model-summary, clinical, availability, and measurement-density features. ResultsThe held-out eICU subset contained 48 positive outcomes (9.39%). The adapted ensemble achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.841 (95% CI 0.775-0.899) and an average precision (AP) of 0.540 (95% CI 0.399-0.676), with a Brier score of 0.063 and expected calibration error of 0.029. The MIMIC-IV source-only stack achieved an AUROC of 0.782; the paired AUROC difference was 0.059 (95% CI 0.003-0.112). In a separate patient-grouped five-fold out-of-fold sensitivity analysis of 503 linked eICU stays, AUROC was 0.807 (95% CI 0.691-0.910). ConclusionsTarget-domain adaptation improved held-out discrimination relative to a source-only stack for sustained antibiotic exposure. Because some model components and measurement-process features used information from the broader first 24 hours, the model should be interpreted as a first-24-hour treatment-association model rather than a strictly prospective pre-treatment infection predictor.

Automated Variant Prioritization in Suspected Genetic Kidney Disease

Rank, N.; Lukassen, S.; Anderegg, M.; Eckardt, K.-U.; Halbritter, J. P.; Popp, B.

IntroductionDespite the identification of >700 genes associated with inherited kidney disease, many affected individuals presumably remain genetically undiagnosed through testing of known disease genes. Novel disease gene discovery requires interpretation of large-scale sequencing data, which remains challenging. MethodsWe developed the Nephro Candidate Score (N-CS), a machine learning (ML) framework for variant prioritization combining a Nephro Gene Score (N-GS), Nephro Variant Score (N-VS), and heuristic Inheritance Score. The ML-based N-GS and N-VS predict gene-disease relevance and variant pathogenicity from diverse genomic features. An English-language proficiency requirement appeared in 5,736 trials (12.9%); its prevalence rose from 7.4% of trials starting in 2010 to 26.2% in 2023 (odds ratio [OR] per decade 2.91, P<0.001), whereas documented accommodation, an eligibility statement allowing participation in a language other than English (Spanish in 99.9% of flagged trials), remained rare (788 trials, 1.8%). The requirement was associated with lower enrolled proportions of Asian (OR=0.85, 95% CI 0.77-0.93, P=0.001) and Hispanic/Latinx participants (OR=0.85, 95% CI 0.79-0.91, P<0.001), with no evidence of an association for White participants (OR=0.99, 95% CI 0.95-1.04, P=0.75). Documented accommodation was associated with a higher proportion of Hispanic/Latinx participants (OR=3.23, 95% CI 2.82-3.71, P<0.001). Geographic adjustment did not change the statistical significance of the requirement associations and attenuated the accommodation association. The main limitations were the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference; the restriction of enrollment analyses to trials reporting baseline demographics, which were more recent than those without such reporting; and possible misclassification of automatically classified exposures. Less common documented language accommodation was associated with a higher enrolled proportion of Hispanic/Latinx participants. Because these criteria are investigator-written and publicly recorded, they may be a tractable target for improving enrollment equity, although whether revising them changes who enrolls requires prospective evaluation.

Personalised normative cardiac phenotyping reveals clinically relevant heterogeneity in cardiac shape and outcome risk

Li, Z.; Bai, W.; Matthews, P. M.; Kainz, B.; Qiao, M.

Standard cardiac biomarkers such as ejection fraction, ventricular volumes, and strain compress complex three-dimensional heart dynamics into a small set of predefined measurements, leaving clinically important heterogeneity unrecognised. Here we propose a personalised normative cardiac frame-work in which each individual is compared to a digital twin representing expected healthy cardiac function given their age, sex, and body habitus. We show that structured patterns of deviation from these normative twins, discovered through systematic exploration of a generative cardiac dynamics space, constitute novel dynamic phenotypes that are not captured by conventional biomarkers. Applied independently to two UK Biobank cardiac cohorts with distinct shape representation, a 128-dimensional four-chamber statistical shape descriptor (HeartSSM, n = 52,365) and a 64-dimensional deep generative latent space from a conditional variational mesh autoencoder (MeshHeart cVAE, n = up to 39,376), we identify reproducible, continuous deviation-from-normative-twin scores (three discrete deviation phenotypes in the HeartSSM cohort) that improve cardiovascular risk stratification beyond ejection fraction, ventricular volumes, myocardial mass, and strain. The strongest effect was seen for incident atrial fibrillation among individuals with preserved ejection fraction ([&ge;]50%): incremental C-index {Delta}C = +0.068 (5-fold cross-validated {Delta}C = +0.063; likelihood-ratio P = 3.3 x 10-8), with a separate, smaller effect for incident stroke in the same preserved-EF subgroup in the independent MeshHeart cVAE representation (which showed no signal for atrial fibrillation) ({Delta}C = +0.024, P = 2.6 x 10-4). Both effects were checked against placebo controls in which the deviation scores were replaced by Gaussian noise. To test whether the normative twin generalises beyond UK Biobanks specific scanning and processing pipeline, we applied the MeshHeart cVAE normative twin, unmodified, to an independent external cohort of healthy volunteers imaged at a different hospital (Gen-Scan/Hammersmith, n = 315): deviation scores in this cohort were of comparable magnitude to the UK Biobank healthy reference (ratio of means = 1.09; Mann-Whitney P = 1.4 x 10-5), indicating that the normative twin captures cross-site-transferable structure rather than a scanner- or pipeline-specific artefact. Our findings suggest that cardiac phenotyping can move beyond predefined measurements toward data-driven dynamic phenotypes that better capture the heterogeneity underlying cardiovascular disease risk, even among individuals whose ejection fraction appears normal.
