Medical Specialty Prediction from Bangla Patient Narratives: A Comparative Study of ML, DL, Transformers, and LLMs

Tasnin Tabassum Ali, Faisal Ahmed, Muhammad Anwarul Azim

Timely access to appropriate medical specialists remains a bottleneck in Bangladesh's healthcare system, as patients describe symptoms in informal Bangla rather than clinical terms, leading to ineffective referrals. This paper compares four model families for predicting medical specialties from Bangla patient narratives: six machine learning (ML) models using hybrid TF-IDF features, four deep learning (DL) models using Word2Vec embeddings trained on the corpus, four fine-tuned transformers, and five large language models (LLMs) evaluated zero-shot. From a corpus of 3798 patient records covering 20 specialties, we retained the five with the most records (Medicine, Gynecology, Dermatology, Pediatrics, Gastroenterology) and split the resulting 2730 records 80:20 into training and test sets shared by all families. The best transformer was XLM-RoBERTa (86.26% accuracy, 86.21% macro-F1), the best ML model was SVM (84.98%, 84.92%), the best DL model was Bidirectional GRU (80.77%, 80.83%), and the best LLM was Gemini (90.29%, 90.15%). Holm-corrected paired McNemar tests show SVM significantly outperforming Bi-LSTM (adjusted p = 0.004), whereas the Gemini-versus-Grok gap does not survive correction (adjusted p = 0.104), and neither the strongest transformer versus the weakest LLM (Grok) nor SVM versus the strongest transformer reaches significance. A tuned classical model is thus statistically indistinguishable from the strongest transformer, so accuracy rankings alone should not be read as evidence of one family's superiority. The findings provide a comparative baseline for Bangla medical text classification.

Continually pretrained open-weight language models can outperform traditional machine learning in EHR-based clinical prediction

Zhou, J.; Zhang, X.; Yan, C.; Ye, J. Z.; Patel, M. B.; Novak, L. L.; Kho, A. N.; Wright, A.; Malin, B. A.; Chen, Y.

Large language models (LLMs) prompted with electronic health record (EHR) data have predicted clinical outcomes less accurately than locally trained machine-learning models. We developed VEX-EHR by extending the vocabulary of a 12-billion-parameter open-weight LLM (weights publicly available) with 17,601 clinical and audit-log codes from 170,483 patients records at one academic medical center, continuing its pretraining on those 2019-2024 records (4.7 billion tokens) and fine-tuning it per task. AUROCs were 0.917 for next-24-hour discharge, 0.715 for 30-day readmission and 0.847 for intensive care unit acute kidney injury, against 0.868, 0.677 and 0.828 for gradient-boosted trees and 0.848, 0.650 and 0.791 for CEHR-BERT, an EHR foundation model. Continued pretraining added 0.031-0.053 AUROC on every task. With 10% of the labels, VEX-EHR exceeded both comparators using all labels for discharge, and CEHR-BERT for readmission, valuable where labels are costly. Adapted by continued pretraining, open-weight LLMs can outperform traditional machine learning in EHR-based prediction.

InterGenEHR$^+$: An Interactive Framework for Natural Language-Conditioned Generation of Longitudinal Patient Trajectories

Yu Yang, Jiafeng Song, Baraa F Abed, Christina Creel, Sagar B Dave, Henry Foote

Objective: To develop and validate InterGenEHR$^+$, an interactive framework for natural language-conditioned generation of synthetic tabular electronic health records (EHRs) and longitudinal trajectories for scenario-based evaluation of machine learning models. Methods: An optional large language model (LLM) parser converts clinical requests into structured constraints. Conditional variational autoencoders and diffusion models generate single-time-point records, while diffusion models generate longitudinal trajectories. We evaluate parsing, distributional fidelity, imputation, clinical-constraint adherence, mortality prediction, and clinical plausibility using a multi-institutional cohort of 874 patients receiving venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO). IGE-TabDDPM and IGE-PMVAE perform well in single-time-point generation; IGE-CSDI, IGE-FGTI, and IGE-MTSCI lead on different longitudinal fidelity metrics. Across five random seeds and three classifiers, synthetic cohorts retain predictive utility for mortality prediction on real test data. Clinician ratings support trajectory plausibility and alignment with requested scenarios. Conclusion: InterGenEHR$^+$ supports clinician-specified generation of EHR trajectories with statistical fidelity and downstream predictive utility in the evaluated VV-ECMO cohort. Extension to other clinical settings requires further validation. Significance: The framework supports scenario-based evaluation of machine learning models in data-sparse critical care settings.

PortalEvent: An Event-Based Ontology for Patient-Initiated Portal Communication

Yang, J.; Gatto, J.; Rahat, R.; Seegmiller, P.; Burdick, T. E.; Keil, M.; Liu, S. K.; Preum, S. M.

BackgroundPatient portal messaging has become a primary channel for asynchronous clinical communication. The messages cover a wide range of topics, from symptom reporting and medication concerns to administrative requests. In recent years, message volume has grown substantially. When a message lacks information the clinician needs, it often leads to back-and-forth that delays resolution of the patients request, and it also takes time from clinicians that could be spent on more urgent matters. To build automated tools that reduce the burden, a structured representation is needed to understand what details these messages contain or lack, so the automated tool can provide help accordingly. However, there is no existing scalable, reproducible method to characterize these messages in a structured way with fine granularity. ObjectiveTo create a scalable, reproducible representation for patient portal communication, we developed an ontology schema grounded in real portal messages and extrinsically validated it by characterizing patient portal messages and clinician follow-up questions in a large electronic health record (EHR) portal dataset from a real health system. MethodsWe developed the ontology through three stages: Literature review, large language model (LLM)-assisted induction and iterative expert review in collaboration with clinical natural language processing (NLP) researchers and three clinicians. We validated the ontology on real patient messages and clinician follow-up questions, and manually verified its results on randomly selected samples. ResultsThe final ontology defines 8 event types and 71 roles covering clinical content (eg, symptoms, medications, diagnostic tests, treatment responses, and patient context) and administrative and social content (medical needs, logistics, and social). In 10,000 randomly selected patient messages, medical needs and symptom events were the most frequent, and clinical and administrative events often co-occurred within a single message. Among 57,321 clinician follow-up questions, the most common targets were symptom, medication, and medical needs events. In manual review, average detection precision was 0.90 or higher for 6 of 8 event types, interannotator agreement on argument judgments exceeded 0.90 for every event type, and detection rates were consistent across 3 open-source LLMs. ConclusionsPortalEvent is a scalable and reproducible event ontology for patient portal messages, developed and validated in collaboration with three clinicians. It can be applied at scale with open-source LLMs in a secure and training-free environment. Results show that messages often combine both clinical and administrative needs, and clinician follow-up questions concentrate on a small set of roles, which frequently seek the basic facts of an event, such as which symptom or medication is involved. These patterns may inform the design of patient- and provider-facing communication support tools.

JHealthBench-L: Clinician-Reviewed Localization of HealthBench for Rubric-Based Medical LLM Evaluation in Japan

Shohei Hisada, Keisuke Orimo, Kenji Igarashi, Kota Sato, Masahiro Kazama, Mitsuhisa Ota

Rubric-based benchmarks such as HealthBench evaluate large language models (LLMs) on open-ended health consultations using case-specific physician criteria, many of which presuppose the healthcare institutions, medications, and care pathways of their source region. Under machine translation alone, these regional assumptions remain in the rubric, leaving responses appropriate in Japan uncredited or falsely penalized under foreign clinical rules. To adapt established clinical evaluation perspectives to Japan without authoring rubrics from scratch, we construct JHealthBench-L (79 patient-facing consultations, 1,022 criteria) through source-cited LLM drafting, single-specialist selection and revision, independent dual-physician review, and meta-review. Specialists rewrote only 4.4% of criteria from scratch, and independent reviewers approved 93.6% of Stage-2 criteria without issue. Comparing JHealthBench-L with a machine-translated baseline across 18 LLMs reveals that translation-only evaluation preserves coarse model rankings ($τ= 0.817$, mean shift +0.027) yet flips verdicts on 17.7% of modified criteria (vs. 7.3% re-grading noise) and reduces false penalties on Japan-adapted negative criteria by 7.1 percentage points, masking item-level clinical mismeasurement behind stable leaderboards. Axis-level and paired analyses further show that context awareness and Japan-specific clinical criteria remain primary bottlenecks, whereas Japanese continual pretraining and reasoning post-training substantially improve locally grounded performance. Datasets, physician revision logs, and evaluation code are available at https://github.com/horshohei/JHealthBench-L.

Beyond billing codes: a proof-of-concept study of large language models for pregnancy episode reconstruction from clinical notes

Schalkamp, A.-K.; Azzam, S.; Ayati, A.; Belov, A.; Wang, M.; Rudrapatna, V. A.

BackgroundAccurate identification of pregnancy episodes from electronic health records (EHRs) is essential for maternal and fetal health research. Existing approaches rely primarily on diagnosis and procedure codes, which may miss pregnancies documented outside the health system and are susceptible to coding errors. Clinical notes contain richer information but remain largely unexplored for this task. ObjectivesTo evaluate whether a large language model (LLM) can reconstruct complete pregnancy episodes directly from clinical notes. MethodsChronologically concatenated notes and messages were provided to GPT-5, which identified episodes and extracted variables including assisted reproductive treatment, gestational age, outcome, and complications. Performance was benchmarked against manual chart review (gold standard) and a hierarchical ICD-code algorithm in 30 patients comprising 74 pregnancies (20 patients in a held-out test set). ResultsThe LLM identified 53 of 54 test-set pregnancies versus 40/54 for a hierarchical ICD-code algorithm, with fewer spurious episodes (3 vs 24), corresponding to an F1 score for pregnancy detection against chart review of 0.96 vs 0.68. It additionally recovered information unavailable in structured data, including reproductive-treatment type and fetus-level outcomes. ConclusionsAn LLM applied to clinical narratives identified pregnancy episodes more completely and with more accurate dates than an ICD-code algorithm while capturing richer clinical detail. Because the two approaches used different input data, these gains reflect both the information in clinical notes and the LLM itself. LLM-based extraction from clinical notes is a promising complement to code-based pregnancy phenotyping that warrants evaluation in larger, multi-institutional cohorts.

MethodsIn this prospective, single-centre, single-arm feasibility study, standard discharge letters were transformed into patient-friendly versions using GPT models, reviewed by treating physicians and provided alongside the originals. Mitigating Hallucinations in Medical Question Answering on ESC Cardiology Guidelines: Evaluation and Retrieval Optimization

Ilkin Masimov, Rufana Baghirli, Muhammad Rashid

Background: Large language models produce hallucinations in medical question answering (QA). Verify-and-revise pipelines that check atomic claims against retrieved guideline evidence are a standard mitigation, but evaluations almost universally treat the verifier's output as ground truth, an assumption rarely tested. Objective: To measure, claim by claim against human judgement, how such a pipeline fails in a guideline-grounded clinical setting. Methods: We built a dataset of 160 questions (100 patient vignettes from 89 de-identified cases, 60 fact-based) over four recent ESC cardiology guidelines, all approved by a practising internist, and a retrieval corpus of 627 hand-verified guideline records at two chunk granularities. A five-stage pipeline was run under four retrieval configurations and three independent runs (1920 arm-runs); all 606 first-pass refuted claims were manually audited, an independent judge re-judged 906 decisions, and the expert blindly reviewed 100 accepted claims. Results: Only 44.7-50.6% of flagged claims were true hallucinations. Correction loops converged in 85.2-95.1% of cases, yet 35% of genuine final errors were introduced by correction. Single-run true-hallucination recall was 35.6-41.2%, the three-run union 71-80%; verifier agreement was moderate (kappa = 0.505). The blind probe estimated the accepted-claim miss rate at 3.2% (95% CI 0.7-6.7%). Final genuine-error rate: 1.7% of answers. Conclusion: Claim-level verification reduces hallucinated content by an order of magnitude, but verifier reliability, not generation, is the limiting factor and must be measured, not assumed.

Can LLM Judges Catch Unsafe Clinical Actions? A Fault-Injection Study

Neda Ghafouri

As clinical AI agents begin to interact with electronic health records (EHRs), large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to evaluate their actions. But how reliably can these judges recognize clinically dangerous mistakes, especially when an agent appears to have completed its task successfully? We investigate this question using MedAgentBench by introducing controlled clinical errors into verified agent trajectories. The experiments include 416 faulty trajectories, eight error categories, and six open-weight LLM judges. By comparing each faulty trajectory with its correct counterpart, we measure whether judges genuinely recognize errors rather than simply flagging more actions as unsafe. The findings show that access to clinical evidence significantly improves judge reliability. When shown only the final answer, judges cannot distinguish errors that leave it unchanged, while flagging 56% of correct trajectories. With full trajectory information, the ability to distinguish unsafe actions from correct ones reaches a score of 0.75, and providing actual EHR responses improves discrimination by 0.39. Surprisingly, including the agent's reasoning does not improve overall performance and reduces detection of incorrectly reported clinical values by 0.18, which remain the hardest errors to detect. MedGemma performed worse than the general-purpose model and was the only judge to miss potentially dangerous medication overdoses. We also find that the benchmark's original grader accepts all 38 trajectories with an intentionally skipped laboratory check, while combining LLM judges with a rule-based checker informed by the full EHR record detects 97% of injected faults.

Laya-IsoToken: Day-One Clinical Codebook Switching via Closed-Form Operator Compilation

Zeqiang Wang

Healthcare systems periodically revise diagnostic and procedural classifications (ICD-9 to ICD-10/11), update hospital reimbursement groupers (MS-DRG), and deploy clinical coding across languages. Supervised coding networks bind clinical text encoders to fixed per-code classifier weights, forcing hospitals to re-annotate tens of thousands of records before switching codebooks, whereas standalone large language models incur high latency and hallucinate invalid codes. Here we show that day-one clinical codebook switching becomes feasible without target-labeled notes when local clinical evidence reading is decoupled from a strictly frozen multilingual concept space. Laya-IsoToken trains a clinical span reader once on source records with zero per-code classifier parameters, separating text evidence from historical disease prevalence. Given only public target code descriptions, closed-form matrix compilation aligns distributional shifts, compensates for paraphrased definitions, and deduplicates sibling codes in under three seconds, while a pre-deployment geometric residual routes unfamiliar concepts to offline language model verification.
