BackgroundMisdiagnosis and delayed diagnosis of children in pediatric emergency departments lead to complications, and are particularly problematic in settings with limited pediatric expertise. Large language models (LLMs) have shown promise in diagnostic reasoning for adult emergency department patients, but utility for pediatric cases is largely untested. MethodsRetrospective study of patients seen in the emergency department of a major urban pediatric academic medical center and subsequently admitted to the operating room or inpatient service. Records from 100 patients were randomly selected, stratified by age. We compared the accuracy of differential diagnoses (up to five, ranked by likelihood) generated by a previously developed agentic AI system, Dr. CaBot (one configuration with literature search, the other without), with those of four attending pediatric emergency medicine physicians (diagnosticians), all masked to outcome. Encounters were presented to diagnosticians stepwise across four touchpoints: triage, the initial physician evaluation (the a priori primary touchpoint), the full emergency department encounter, and the admission. Ground truth diagnosis was determined by two independent, board-certified pediatric emergency physicians (reviewers) based on the entire clinical encounter. The reviewers, masked to source, then assigned each differential a Bond score (0 to 5; 4 or above was high-scoring). FindingsThe median patient age was 4 years and 41% had a complex chronic condition. Bond score agreement between the two physician reviewers had a weighted kappa of 0.64. For the primary comparison at the initial physician evaluation touchpoint, Dr. CaBot and the diagnosticians both achieved high-scoring differentials in 95% of cases (difference 0%, 95%CI: -5.8, 5.8%). No differences were detected at the other touchpoints. InterpretationDr. CaBot and attending pediatric emergency medicine physicians showed similar performance in generating differential diagnoses from clinical documentation, suggesting potential as a diagnostic aid. Whether this performance can be translated into improvements in care requires prospective evaluation. OmniTongue: A Comprehensive MLLM Benchmark for Automated Tongue Diagnosis in Traditional Chinese Medicine

Liuyi Xu, Yun Guo, Shusheng Li, Xuewen Zhang, Yining Qian, An-Yang Lu

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have excelled in Western medical imaging, yet their potential in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) tongue diagnosis remains systematically unevaluated. Tongue diagnosis requires sophisticated global and local perception, serving as a rigorous touchstone for assessing the visual understanding depth of MLLMs. However, existing datasets are limited by single dimensions, coarse granularity, and ambiguous labels, hindering the evaluation of authentic clinical capabilities. To bridge this gap, we introduce OmniTongue, the first comprehensive, fine-grained Visual Question Answering benchmark for TCM tongue diagnosis. This benchmark integrates 1,351 expert-validated images and 21,616 instruction pairs. Its core design features 16 clinical dimensions for multidimensional evaluation, region-specific annotations for pathological localization, and 66 fine-grained labels for precise diagnostic representation. Extensive evaluations of existing MLLMs reveal significant performance gaps, demonstrating that medical-specific models lack domain advantages, while universally poor Macro-F1 scores expose a reliance on statistical shortcuts rather than genuine understanding of tongue pathology. These findings not only highlight the current limitations of MLLMs in interpreting regional medical features but also underscore OmniTongue's significance for developing robust automated tongue diagnosis models. The code and dataset will be made publicly available in a future release.

Toward a digital twin of the ICU patient: Forecasting and treatment response in a generative event model

Zhuoyang Lyu, Yiming Li, Peter C. Hou, Pengyu Hong, Li Zhou

A digital twin of a patient in intensive care would forecast the patient's course and what a treatment would change. Generative event models, trained to continue a patient's electronic health record (EHR), have been proposed as a path toward such twins. We trained TWICE, a 79M-parameter decoder-only transformer, on the ICU records of one academic health system (127,332 training patients and 1.57 billion tokens). We evaluated it on clinical prediction tasks, generation fidelity, individual trajectories and counterfactual validation. Reading 19 clinical outcomes off 50 simulated futures, with no task-specific training, TWICE matched the best of 144 supervised configurations on held-out patients (mean AUROC 0.836 vs. 0.834). It led on events that change care within a day and trailed on death. With the training data fixed, more and longer simulated futures were worth more than a 6.8-fold larger model. Its generated records matched real ones closely and were as specific to each patient as its own variability allowed. Its predicted risk rose as events approached. Pre-specified controls showed that it reproduced the side effects of drugs and was barely moved by an inert drug. On the values that treatments are given to correct, however, an opposite treatment moved its forecasts like the right one. TWICE forecasts ICU events about as well as task-specific models, and we see it as a step toward a digital twin of the ICU patient.

TabBioMed: A Large-Scale Benchmark for Biomedical Tabular Learning

Pau Mateo-Bernadó, Saivenkata Nagavyjayanthi Polapragada, David Bonet, Pol Arbiol Rakuljic, Laia Mogas Pladevall, Marçal Comajoan Cara

Recent advances in machine learning have accelerated biomedical prediction, but evaluation on structured biomedical data remains fragmented, especially for datasets with high dimensionality, large sample size, missingness, and class imbalance. We introduce TabBioMed, a large-scale benchmark of 94 curated public biomedical tabular datasets spanning electronic health records, drug discovery, genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, single-cell omics, and systems biology. TabBioMed unifies these datasets through a standardized, dataset-aware preprocessing and evaluation framework, enabling reproducible comparison across classification, regression, and multi-target tasks. We benchmark 23 models across classical baselines, gradient-boosted trees, neural tabular architectures, and tabular foundation models. Foundation models deliver the strongest family-level predictive performance. However, their gains are regime-dependent: tree-based models and tuned multilayer perceptrons remain highly competitive, often dominating under inference-time or training-cost constraints. Overall, TabBioMed shows that biomedical tabular learning is strongly context-dependent, with optimal model choice depending on data regime, task type, and computational budget. We release TabBioMed with curated datasets and splits, preprocessing code, baselines, and results, providing a reproducible benchmark for practical model selection and future methods development in biomedical machine learning, available at https://github.com/AI-sandbox/TabBioMed.

Leakage-Free Is Not Confounder-Free: What a Patient-Level Split Cannot Certify About a Medical Imaging Benchmark

Ignazio Gallo, Mattia Gatti, Leonardo Sgroi, Darwin Castillo, Yuliana Jiménez-Gaona, Nicola Landro

Patient-level splitting is the standard safeguard against subject leakage in medical imaging evaluation, but it cannot remove a confounder that every fold inherits: an acquisition variable aligned with the diagnosis across the whole cohort. We present a five-question audit of a candidate confounder: how well it predicts the diagnosis, whether it can be decoded from the images, whether diagnostic scores depend on it, whether the image adds predictive value beyond it and how much discrimination survives once it is controlled. We apply it to DMR-IR, the public dataset on which most machine-learning results in breast thermography rest. On 236 patients under a strict patient-disjoint protocol, the acquisition date alone predicts malignancy at AUROC 0.980, above the best of nine imaging arms (0.949), because benign and malignant patients were recruited in different years. The acquisition year is decodable from the pixels among patients who share a diagnosis, largely through a global thermal offset outside the breast, yet only one arm, the one pooling every frame of a visit, demonstrably carries it into its diagnostic score. No arm improves on the date on paired folds, and once the date is controlled, discrimination either collapses or survives only on comparisons the benchmark cannot attribute. The dataset contains only eight cross-label same-date pairs, from ten patients, which limits reliable evaluation within recorded acquisition-date groups. A second, independently acquired cohort, whose dominant channel is patient age, returns the same audit outcome from a different confounder. Mitigation may suppress such a confounder, but without cross-label acquisition support no evaluation on the benchmark can certify that what remains is pathology.

ObjectiveBiopsies are examined under the microscope by a pathologist, giving a diagnosis, which guides treatment and predicts prognosis. These challenges have motivated us to develop an artificial intelligence (AI)-mediated approach for the automated diagnosis of up to 80% of duodenal biopsies (i.e., most cases of the two most frequent diagnoses: coeliac disease and "normal"). Methods and AnalysisWe conducted mixed-methods stakeholder engagement (February 2024 to June 2026). MedicalRSI: Medical Recursive Self-Improving Agents via Clinical Model-Harness Co-Evolution

Ziqing Wang, Hamid Palangi, Lili Zhao, Kaize Ding

Medical agents built on large language models (LLMs) have advanced rapidly, yet most stay fixed once deployed and do not keep using their experience to improve later attempts. As a result, the same kind of error can recur across patients. To address this, existing self-improvement methods update an agent's weights or its medical harness from its own experience, but their improving strategy stays fixed and never learns which changes actually help, which caps how much the agent can improve. Recursive self-improvement (RSI) instead improves both the agent and its improving strategy. However, achieving RSI for medical agents is challenging, because clinical workflows rarely come with a reference harness, and a change that fixes early errors may not fix the problems the agent meets later. We propose MedicalRSI, an RSI framework built on Clinical Model-Harness Co-Evolution, in which one model plays two roles. The Solver is the medical agent itself, and the Improver is its learnable improving strategy, which writes a medical harness from the agent's failures. Each harness is rewarded by its gain over acting without it, and both roles are trained together, so the Improver keeps adapting to the problems the Solver has not yet solved. On multiple benchmarks and LLM backbones, MedicalRSI outperforms existing context-level and weight-level self-improvement methods and transfers well to unseen workflows, new LLMs and new databases, a step toward medical agents that keep improving.

Beyond Final Answers: Interpretable Tumor Immune Microenvironment Reasoning from H&E and mIF

Yixuan Duan, Kexin Geng, Hanwen Xu, Wei Qiu

Readouts of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), such as CD8+ T-cell density, intra-epithelial infiltration and the Ki67 index, combine tissue architecture from H&E with cell phenotypes from multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF). Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) could automate these readouts with an explanation, but two gaps remain. Pathology MLLMs read H&E alone, while these readouts need the cell phenotypes that mIF measures. And their reasoning has no step-level truth to be checked against, so R1-style reinforcement learning rewards only the final answer and leaves the reasoning unverified. Same-section H&E and mIF provide what both gaps lack: mIF measures every cell without manual annotation, which gives cell-level ground truth for each step that clinical guidelines prescribe, from delineating a tissue compartment to counting and normalizing the marker-positive cells within it. We therefore present HERMES (H&E-mIF Reasoning with Multimodal Evidence Supervision), an R1-style MLLM that reasons jointly over paired H&E and mIF in guideline steps and is trained with a process reward that checks every stated quantity against this truth. Across four field-level TIME tasks in colorectal cancer, HERMES (4B) reaches an average accuracy of 84.5%, compared with 74.7% for the strongest of twelve proprietary, general, medical and pathology MLLMs, and a higher share of its intermediate quantities is correct than for the strongest open-weight baseline. Without the process reward, reinforcement learning barely improves on supervised fine-tuning (80.0% vs. 78.8%) and fewer stated quantities are correct; editing a decisive stated quantity changes the answer as implied in all 885 test questions. Without retraining, it keeps the highest average accuracy on an external cohort imaged with CODEX (76.4% vs. 67.3%) and on head and neck cancer, a cancer type absent from training (76.7% vs. 72.5%).

Learning 3D MRI Representations with Generative Adversarial Networks

Sujeet Lahane, Sapna Mishra, Akash Suryavanshi, Tapan Gandhi, Bharat Biswal

Learning representations of visual images is a challenging task. It requires extracting abstract patterns from high-dimensional data without explicit supervision. This becomes pronounced with 3D MR imaging due to sparse features, higher dimensional inputs, and smaller datasets. Many state-of-the-art representation learning algorithms successfully rely on transformers and diffusion-based architectures. However, these methods struggle with smaller datasets. In effect, solution methods relax the problem by slicing 3D volumes and solving the problem for 2D medical imaging, resulting in a loss of spatial 3D information. We present R2RG, a representation learning method based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), with Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) honing the reconstruction task. Our key insights lie in exploiting architectural biases in 3D CNNs, combined with data-efficient auto-encoding GANs and a composite loss function to enhance reconstruction fidelity and stabilize training. Our experiments on three standard datasets and a locally collected dataset show that R2RG not only achieves superior performance but also generalizes to heterogeneous and smaller datasets. Furthermore, representations learned with R2RG outperform those learned with contrastive-based representation learning methods on Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease classification. Our work demonstrates that reconstruction-based representation learning is a promising direction for 3D medical imaging under limited data availability.

Multi-Tier Edge Triage: Optimizing Latency for Safe Clinical Agents

Joshua Broyde, Sarita A. Joshi

Deploying generative AI agents in enterprise clinical workflows requires enforcing multi-dimensional safety and compliance policies--preventing HIPAA Protected Health Information (PHI) exposure, maintaining clinical scope-of-practice boundaries, ensuring patient-appropriate reading comprehension, and preserving brand integrity--under interactive latency constraints. Synchronous outbound calls to cloud Large Language Model (LLM) judges add substantial network and decoding overhead onto the critical response path. We present Multi-Tier Edge Triage, a safety screening architecture that co-locates a compact Tier-1 sequence classifier on the clinical agent's host server, resolving clear queries in-process without a network round-trip and escalating only ambiguous queries to a Tier-2 cloud LLM judge. To evaluate Tier-1 encoders without length or unigram shortcuts, we construct four length-matched contrastive twin benchmarks seeded from MedSafetyBench, ASQ-PHI, and a synthetic four-mandate clinical assistant policy (Generic HealthCorp). While frozen local encoders match cloud models on structured PHI detection (>= 98.3% accuracy in <= 23.5 ms), they degrade markedly on semantic safety and symmetric-blocklist prompts. However, end-to-end representation fine-tuning closes the multi-policy accuracy gap: a fine-tuned 335M-parameter BGE-Large classifier achieves 98.90% out-of-fold accuracy versus 99.20% for a zero-shot cloud LLM judge (exact McNemar p = 0.648, zero shared errors), 97.8%-98.2% recall, and a 0.0%-0.4% over-refusal rate in 39.0 ms (>14x faster than cloud LLM evaluation on our benchmark host). Per-policy error analysis reveals ceiling local recall on PHI (100.0%), brand (100.0%), and clinical scope (98.7%-99.3%) violations, isolating residual errors to reading-level checks (93.3%-95.3%) and motivating targeted multi-tier escalation.

Neurodegenerative Differential Diagnosis with Knowledge-enhanced Structural Reasoning

Mingxuan Wang, Siyuan Du, Yanfeng Wang, Ya Zhang, Chencheng Zhang, Jiangchao Yao

Accurate differential diagnosis of neurodegenerative disorders remains challenging due to overlapping clinical manifestations and heterogeneous, often incomplete multimodal observations. Existing multimodal models can integrate imaging and clinical measurements but often overlook disease-specific evidence patterns and pairwise relations among competing diagnoses. We therefore propose Knowledge-enhanced Structural Reasoning (KnowSR), which aligns multimodal patient representations with a literature-grounded knowledge bank of disease prototypes, signed evidence directions, and pairwise diagnostic relations. Specifically, in KnowSR, patient-specific knowledge alignment and target-aware pooling inject structured disease evidence, while anchored relational reasoning reconciles pairwise diagnostic margins over a sparse differential graph, thereby collectively enhancing differential diagnostic capability. Across extensive experiments, KnowSR consistently outperforms 19 baselines on patient-level six-class diagnosis under patient-disjoint five-fold evaluation. This shows that grounding patient evidence in structured medical knowledge enables more effective differential diagnosis than naive multimodal fusion alone.

Scalable Evaluation of Medical AI using Evidence-based Practice Recommendations

Teya Bergamaschi, Bijal Rajput, Akshay Goel, Matthew Thompson, Jacqueline Shreibati, Dave Steiner

Rigorously evaluating medical artificial intelligence (AI) requires testing open-ended clinical management across diverse presentations and stages of care.
