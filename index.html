Identifying interstitial lung disease with large language models on clinical notes and claims

Larghero, G.; Schneeweiss, S.; Gu, B.; Anand, P.; Lin, J. K.; Yang, J.

BackgroundInterstitial lung disease (ILD) is a potentially fatal toxicity of immune check-point inhibitors and antibody-drug conjugates. Drug-safety studies in healthcare databases identify ILD with claims-based algorithms that trade positive predictive value (PPV) against sensitivity. We assessed whether large language models (LLMs) improve on these algorithms, with and without electronic health record (EHR) notes, and whether these gains translate into measurable epidemiological benefits. MethodsIn 309 oncology patients with ILD diagnosis codes at Mass General Brigham, adjudicated by two pulmonologists, we compared 12 open-access LLMs with 20 published claims-based algorithms. LLMs received claims, EHR notes, or both, without task-specific training. Measures were PPV and yield (proportion classified positive). We projected the attenuation of a drug-induced ILD odds ratio under non-differential outcome misclassification. FindingsLLMs extended the PPV-yield frontier of the published algorithms with all three inputs. Across the 36 model-input evaluations, LLM PPV exceeded that of the best algorithm without specialist-coding requirements (Algorithm 4) by 14.3 percentage points on average. With claims alone, Qwen3-8B achieved 89.8% PPV (95% CI 78.2-95.6) at 15.9% yield, versus 86.7% at 4.9% for the highest-PPV algorithm. With notes alone, Qwen3-32B achieved 78.9% PPV at 49.2% yield, versus 64.6% at 51.1% for Algorithm 4. With combined claims and notes, Qwen3-14B achieved 100.0% PPV at 24.3% yield. Under a simulated drug-safety scenario with a true odds ratio of 3.0, the projected observed odds ratio was 2.67 with combined inputs versus 1.07 with Algorithm 4, corresponding to a 46-fold difference in the estimated sample size required for 80% power (591 versus 27,200 patients). InterpretationZero-shot LLMs offer a higher-PPV alternative to rule-based claims pheno-typing for ILD, on all three input types. Because the claims-only configuration uses the same claims data as existing algorithms, it could be deployed in administrative databases without linked notes, and may recover drug-safety signal otherwise lost to outcome misclassification. FundingNational Institute on Aging, National Library of Medicine, PCORI, and the National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases. Research in contextO_ST_ABSEvidence before this studyC_ST_ABSWe searched PubMed up to August 2026 for studies on claims or electronic health record (EHR)-based identification of interstitial lung disease and on machine-learning or large language model (LLM) phenotyping of clinical conditions. Search terms included "interstitial lung disease or pneumonitis", "administrative claims", "computational phenotyping", "rule-based algorithms", "machine learning", and "large language models". We also screened the reference lists of relevant articles. The literature search suggested that identifying ILD in longitudinal claims data is unreliable because the relevant diagnosis codes are nonspecific, and validated rule-based algorithms face a structural tradeoff between PPV and coverage. Supervised machine-learning classifiers have outperformed rule-based ILD identification on electronic health record data, and zero-shot LLMs have already shown the ability to phenotype chronic conditions from structured codes. No study, however, had compared a zero-shot LLM head-to-head against validated, published claims-based algorithms for a specific, high-stakes drug-safety outcome on a single fixed cohort, isolated the contribution of the input substrate from that of the model, or quantified the downstream consequence for pharmacoepidemiologic effect estimation. Added value of this studyOn a single, fixed, expert-adjudicated cohort, the evaluated LLMs achieved higher positive predictive value (PPV) than all 20 published claims-based algorithms at comparable yield (the proportion of patients classified as ILD-positive), whether the models received structured claims, clinical notes, or both. Across the 36 model-input evaluations, PPV exceeded that of the most inclusive published rule by 19.7 percentage points on average, and did so in 33 of 36 evaluations. We show that clinical notes generally increased yield, while the resulting PPV-yield trade-off depended on the model and input combination. Restricting the input to a single EHR note type preserved the advantage over rule-based phenotyping in matched comparisons on the patients possessing that EHR note type. Most consequentially, this improvement reduces the sample size required to detect a hypothesised drug-ILD association from approximately 27,200 patients for the best existing algorithm for the task to 591 when using LLMs, and offer a deployable upgrade for the note-free administrative databases where rule-based algorithms are the current standard. Implications of all the available evidenceFor pharmacovigilance of treatment-related ILD, the limiting step is often not the size of the available claims database but the misclassification that erodes the very signal under surveillance. Replacing rule-based claims phenotyping algorithms with zero-shot LLM-based alternatives transforms a high-PPV, low-yield classifier into one that recovers many more patients at the same PPV, converting a classifier that can render drug-safety associations statistically invisible into one that makes them detectable in cohorts of feasible size. Pending external validation, this offers a practical route to more sensitive post-marketing surveillance of the pulmonary toxicity of immune checkpoint inhibitors and antibody-drug conjugates. More broadly, we hypothesise that this advantage is not specific to ILD: zero-shot LLM phenotyping may recover drug-safety signal for other outcomes that diagnosis codes capture poorly but that are well documented in clinical text, a possibility that warrants dedicated evaluation.

Findings-aware Language-Image Pretraining for CT Volume Interpretation

Takuro Shimaya, Junya Inoh, Masahiro Saiko

Computed tomography (CT) provides diverse patient information and various clinical tasks, for which numerous deep learning models have been investigated. Recent years have seen the development of vision-language foundation models that can comprehensively extract clinical features, driven by representation learning that combines CT volumes with radiology reports. Existing approaches typically adopt Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP)-based representation learning, where pairs from different cases are, in principle, treated as negative pairs such that the volume and report embeddings are pushed apart. In the biomedical imaging, however, treating different cases as negative pairs is not always appropriate, particularly when clinical similarity exists. In this study, we propose a supervised representation learning framework grounded in clinical knowledge from language foundation models, rather than the weak supervision used in CLIP. Specifically, we utilized a large language model to extract abnormal finding phrases from reports. We then explicitly constructed the target features that the image encoder should output, as the semantic representation of the extracted findings using a biomedical text embedding model. Our Findings-aware Language-Image Pre-training (FiLIP) outperformed weakly supervised approaches in multi-label zero-shot finding classification in both in-domain and external-domain evaluations, and also proved effective when fine-tuned for the downstream task of radiology report generation.

Benchmarking Large Language Models on Heterogeneous Cognitive and Biomedical Knowledge Graphs: The Role of Graph Grounding in Multi-Hop Reasoning and Hallucination Mitigation

Ali Sarabadani, Kheirolah Rahsepar Fard, Tahereh Jeirani

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in biomedical and cognitive neuroscience, but their ability to reason reliably over heterogeneous knowledge graphs (KGs) remains unclear. We benchmark five LLMs (GPT-5.1, Gemini 2.0 Pro, Llama 3, Qwen, and BioGPT) across five KGs: DrugBank, NeuroSynth, Cognitive Atlas, Brain Map, and GDPKG. We introduce a multitier query dataset to evaluate reasoning depth and factual accuracy under KG-aware (graph-grounded) and KG-agnostic conditions. Inter-annotator agreement was substantial, with Cohen's kappa of 0.781 (range: 0.753-0.807) and Fleiss' kappa of 0.77. Results show that general-purpose models, particularly GPT-5.1 and Gemini 2.0 Pro, generally outperform domain-specific models on multi-hop and high-level reasoning tasks, although performance declines as query complexity increases. KG grounding reduces hallucination rates across all evaluated models. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, domain-specific models do not consistently outperform general-purpose models on specialized tasks without graph grounding. These findings highlight the value of combining broad language-model capabilities with structured domain knowledge. We provide practical guidance for selecting LLM-KG integration strategies based on task complexity and computational constraints, and discuss hybrid neurosymbolic architectures for personalized medicine, cognitive disorder diagnosis, and explainable AI.

Multidimensional Suicide Risk Assessment with Machine Learning using Electronic Health Records

Nathan Scollar, Kemal Davaslioglu, Nathan Conger, Sastry Kompella, Yalin E. Sagduyu, Nathaniel D. Bastian

Suicide prevention remains a critical public health challenge, and electronic health records (EHRs) provide an opportunity to characterize suicide-related risk indicators using documented clinical, behavioral, medical, and social-contextual information. This paper presents a multidimensional machine learning framework to model and estimate suicide-related outcomes using MIMIC-IV EHR data. We construct clinically motivated diagnosis categories and define a composite outcome from documented codes corresponding to suicidal ideation, suicide attempt, prior suicidal behavior, and intentional self-harm. To address severe class imbalance and reduce age-related confounding, we apply age-matched resampling that retains all positive patients and samples comparison-cohort patients from the same age group. Logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost classifiers are evaluated before and after resampling with isotonic calibration. The calibrated XGBoost model achieves the strongest matched-cohort performance, outperforming the other evaluated classifiers. SHAP-based interpretation shows that diagnostic burden, depression-related features, age, personality-disorder history, social determinants related to violence, and medical comorbidity contribute to elevated predicted probabilities. Our results demonstrate that combining broad EHR-derived features, age-matched cohort construction, calibration, and feature-level interpretation provides a practical framework for retrospective suicide-related risk modeling.

Learning a patient world model for health system simulation in intensive care

Yingtai Li, Ruoyan Wang, Yu Li, Shaohua Kevin Zhou

Designing healthcare strategies requires anticipating their clinical and operational consequences before implementation, a task that often relies on health system simulation. Such a simulation necessitates modeling how patient states evolve and generate care demands. However, conventional approaches typically tailor compact state transitions to a single policy question and omit history-dependent dynamics, thus requiring the redefinition of states and transitions when there is a new question that entails different clinical information. We propose to learn a patient world model from longitudinal EHRs using a neural network to provide a shared clinical representation of patient trajectories that can serve simulation questions across scenarios. Taking ICU operations as an example, we train the model on MIMIC-IV, freeze it, and couple its time-queryable predictions to shared ICU calendars, resources, and queues via scenario-specific mappings, so that one model renders admission demand, care tasks, and discharge proposals across simulations evaluating ICU bed capacity, care dispatch, and post-ICU transfer capacity. Experiments show that our model matches Markov and semi-Markov comparators in aggregate occupancy and directional responses to capacity and dispatch policies. It also reproduces policy gains from replayed observed trajectories. For patients entering the same support state from different prior trajectories, the learned representation captures a 24-h recovery difference (-6.4 predicted versus -6.7 observed percentage points), a contrast missed by compact state models, which propagates into distinct simulated care demand, queue burden, and bed use. These results indicate that a learned world model of patient dynamics has the potential to replace question-specific state models, allowing new care policies to be evaluated by reconfiguring the scenario without model rebuilding.

NPCBench: A Clinical Apprenticeship Benchmark for Multimodal Care-Pathway Reasoning in Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma

Pengkai Wang, Wei-Wei Zhang, Yan Li, Min Tang, Zhitian Hou, Zeyu Liu

Current medical benchmarks typically break down clinical competence into isolated factual questions, single-image tasks, or static case vignettes. These tests score individual answers, but reveal less about whether a model can integrate subspecialty knowledge with multimodal evidence and track a patient's condition throughout a course of care. To close this gap, we introduce NPCBench, a clinical apprenticeship benchmark for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). NPCBench mirrors specialist training, from guideline and foundational knowledge to case planning, longitudinal review, CarePath integration, and open-ended consultation. It contains 4,777 component and consultation items and a 100-patient CarePath cohort with 417 scored diagnostic and care tasks. The episode-level evaluation tests whether diagnostic and care answers are jointly correct within a care pathway. By linking stepwise tasks to patient-level care trajectories, NPCBench evaluates whether models ground decisions in visual evidence, interpret changes in patient state, and meet clinical requirements in consultation. Across 30 evaluated models, we observe a composition gap: strong performance on individual tasks does not ensure jointly correct diagnostic and care answers for the same patient. Among 21 multimodal models, strict CarePath success reaches at most 23%. Paired controls and physician calibration further examine evidence grounding, longitudinal revision, and consultation judging. Overall, NPCBench broadens the evaluation of medical artificial intelligence beyond static question-answering paradigms to an apprenticeship-oriented assessment of clinical workflows, thereby offering a testbed for evidence-based and temporally coherent reasoning in oncology care.

How Deep is the Signal? Time-Context up to 30 Years for Colorectal Cancer Prediction in a Primary-Care EHR Model

Achille Raviez, Lan Wang, Brendan Delaney

EHR foundation models read medical events within a fixed context window, and long context has so far been measured only in tokens. How far back in time a model must look to capture the earliest predictive signal is unknown. We introduce time-context, the calendar lookback window before the prediction point, and provide, to our knowledge, the first systematic evaluation of decade-scale time-context for an EHR foundation model. We pretrained a decoder-only transformer (34.6M parameters) on 1.4B coded events from 3.6M primary-care patients in North West London and fine-tuned it to predict incident colorectal cancer under a 12-month pre-diagnostic buffer. A frozen cohort of 1,008 cases and 9,582 matched controls, all registered for at least 30 years, was evaluated at 15 nested lookback windows from 2 to 30 years, so that every truncation is compared within the same patients. Discrimination rose from an AUROC of 0.551 at 2 years to 0.590 at 18 years, then plateaued, with about 80% of the gain reached within 10 years; an XGBoost baseline converged to the same level (peak AUROC 0.589). A simulation further suggests that rotary position embeddings (RoPE) over elapsed time alias beyond about 20 years at the default base frequency, motivating a retuned $θ$. Time-context is a measurable design axis for clinical prediction models, distinct from token-context, and should be reported.

Variantscape: Retrospective Study of Large Language Model-Based Extraction of Variant-Treatment-Cancer Associations from Published Oncology Abstracts

Wosny, M.; Blindu, A. S.; Boesch, M.; Peres, T.; Niederhauser, T.; Fruh, M.; Rothermundt, C.; Hastings, J.

ObjectivesPrecision oncology relies on accurate interpretation of tumour-detected gene variants, to guide personalised treatment decisions. However, interpretation of variants in context requires extensive information that is often buried within unstructured biomedical literature and obscured by inconsistent nomenclature, making manual retrieval labour-intensive and prone to omissions. In this study, our aim was to evaluate the potential of large language models to extract variant-treatment-cancer associations at scale from published abstracts. DesignWe developed Variantscape, a large-scale, automated pipeline and open-access web tool. It integrates traditional natural language processing methods with state-of-the-art large language models to extract, standardise, and analyse co-associations between genetic variants, cancer types, and therapeutic interventions from published biomedical abstracts. ResultsFrom over 3.7 million abstracts published between January 2014 and October 2026, 886,195 articles containing gene names were eligible for extraction. Among these, 37,895 (4.3%) mentioned a variant, cancer type, and therapeutic agent together, covering 11,985 unique variants across 197 cancer types and 547 therapeutic agents. LLM verification against the abstract text confirmed at least one association in 35,224 papers, giving a literature-derived graph of 10,571 nodes and 44,644 verified edges. Canonical alterations in well-studied cancers (e.g., BRAF V600E in melanoma) were strongly linked to established treatments, while several rare variants also emerged with high-confidence literature support. ConclusionsBy applying large language models to biomedical literature, Variantscape enables scalable, context-aware extraction of trilateral variant-treatment-cancer relationships. This approach supports early evidence synthesis/hypothesis generation, highlights underrecognized or rare associations for MTB preparation, and offers a practical resource for accelerating discovery and supporting precision oncology research and translation. FundingThis study was funded by the School of Medicine at the University of St. Gallen in Switzerland under grant number 2300380. MedGraph: Maintaining Patient State with Graph Memory for Long-Term Medical Dialogue

Donghua Zhang, Zizheng Wang, Ze Dong, Yiding Feng, Siyu Qian

LLM-based medical assistants that follow a patient across visits need a memory that keeps the patient's clinical state correct as it changes. We examine the errors of five memory systems on longitudinal medical dialogue and find three recurring causes: the retrieved evidence gives an outdated state, includes another person's health facts, or lacks a safety fact the answer depends on. We present MedGraph, a graph memory built for these three cases. Construction extracts dated facts from each session, attributes each fact to the person it describes, and links medication, diagnosis, and measurement records into chains that keep earlier states. Retrieval ranks the patient's chains as units, reads each chain at the time the question asks about, and adds the original dialogue text; a patient profile lists every recorded safety fact with every question. On MedMemoryBench, MedGraph has the highest overall accuracy with both a GPT and a Qwen backbone, 10.5 and 10.4 points above the strongest of five baselines, with efficient memory construction and answering in the GPT setting.

CardioLM: A multilingual suite of small language models for the cardiology domain

Manuela-Daniela Danu, Miguel Rodríguez-Ortega, Alberto Becerra-Tome, Pablo Arancibia, Fernando Gallego-Donoso, Lorenzo Pratesi

Background: Cardiovascular care generates large volumes of clinical free-text documentation in local languages. However, clinical natural language processing (NLP) resources remain concentrated in English, and no existing benchmark is cardiology-specific, multilingual, and broad in entity coverage. Methods: We present two contributions. The first is CardioCCC, a corpus of 508 cardiology case reports originally published in Spanish and extended to Czech, Dutch, English, Italian, Romanian, and Swedish through machine translation, annotation projection, and clinician review, with annotations covering diseases, symptoms, procedures, and medications. The second is CardioLM, comprising CardioBERTa, a set of language-specific encoders obtained by continued pretraining on cardiology and biomedical corpora, and CardioNER, a set of named entity recognition (NER) models fine-tuned on CardioCCC. Results: Cardiology-specific pretraining reduced pseudo-perplexity by up to a factor of eight, yet changed strict span-level F1 by at most 0.04, with benefits concentrated in languages whose backbones lacked biomedical coverage. The jointly trained multilingual XLM-RoBERTa-large models achieved the best strict F1 in nearly every language and entity type, exceeding the strongest monolingual system by up to 0.13 F1. A large share of residual errors concerned boundary detection: relaxed matching recovered 0.10-0.23 F1 for disease, procedure, and symptom, but only 0.01-0.07 for medication. Clinicians rated CardioNER well above a rule- and dictionary-based baseline, with the mean rating across entity types rising from 26% to 73%. Conclusions: Specialty-specific pretraining is not the decisive factor for downstream entity recognition, whereas multilingual joint training yields the strongest results in nearly all settings and a simpler deployment path for multi-country clinical information extraction.

MIMIC-Note-Extract: A Large-Scale Structured Dataset Extracted from MIMIC-IV-Note Discharge Summaries

Kristian Schwethelm, Jonas Kuntzer, Conrad Ketzer, Eva Wende, Moritz Schlager, Daniel Rueckert

MIMIC is one of the most widely used public hospital datasets for machine learning on electronic health records, yet clinically important information, such as past medical history, allergies, and diagnostic findings, remains confined to free-text discharge summaries. Studies requiring this information must therefore build their own extraction pipelines, duplicating substantial effort across projects. We release MIMIC-Note-Extract (MNE), a large-scale dataset that uses a hybrid extraction pipeline combining deterministic parsing with schema-guided large language model generation to transform 16 sections from 300,720 discharge summaries into structured tables. MNE adds information that has no structured MIMIC counterpart and enriches related tables with broader coverage and greater clinical detail, including 10 times more patient-history entries and medication histories for more than twice as many admissions. Validation by medical doctors confirms extraction accuracy above 92% across sections. Using MNE, we construct a clinical decision-making benchmark for LLM agents, demonstrating how reusable structured representations of clinical narratives reduce data curation effort.
